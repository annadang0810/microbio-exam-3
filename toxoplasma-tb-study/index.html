The Big Picture

Lecture 23 expands the intracellular pathogen story with two organisms that share one trait — both live inside host cells — but otherwise sit at biological extremes:

  • Toxoplasma gondii: a single-celled parasite with a complex life cycle that requires cats for sexual reproduction but multiplies asexually in nearly any warm-blooded animal. Forms cysts in tissues and oocysts in cat feces.
  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis: an acid-fast bacterium (mycolic-acid-coated, neither Gram positive nor negative) that lives inside alveolar macrophages, blocks phagosome-lysosome fusion, delays adaptive immunity, and triggers granuloma formation during latency.

Connection Map

Both pathogens establish lifelong infection in immunocompetent hosts and reactivate when immunity wanes — one of the major reasons HIV/AIDS, chemotherapy, and immune suppression unmask these "quiet" infections in dramatic ways.

Why these matter together

  • Both use a vacuole — T. gondii in the parasitophorous vacuole (PV); M. tb in the phagosome (which it prevents from maturing into a phagolysosome)
  • Both infect macrophages — key innate immune cells that should be killing them
  • Both manipulate host cell death — T. gondii tachyzoites lyse the cell to spread; M. tb exploits macrophage apoptosis for cell-to-cell transfer inside the granuloma
  • Both have huge global prevalence — ~11% of Americans / 60% of Brazilians latently infected with T. gondii; ~1/3 of the global population (~2.7 billion) latently infected with M. tb

Toxoplasma gondii: The Organism HIGH YIELD

T. gondii Basics

  • Single-celled parasite
  • Can infect almost any cell type in mammals and birds
  • Causes the disease Toxoplasmosis in humans
  • The genus Toxoplasma contains only one species — T. gondii — with multiple genotypes and haplotypes
  • Over 40 million Americans may be infected (~11%)
  • Worldwide: up to 60% in Brazil
  • Transmission: consumption of raw or undercooked meat (containing tissue cysts), contaminated water/soil/produce (sporulated oocysts), or cat feces
  • Infections can persist for life
  • Risk groups: immunocompromised individuals and pregnant women

Toxoplasmosis — Disease Spectrum

Tachyzoite Cell Architecture HIGH YIELD

The tachyzoite is the most well characterized stage. It has multiple apical secretory organelles — specialized structures clustered at the front of the cell that mediate host cell invasion:

OrganelleWhat it does
MicronemesSecrete proteins with perforin-like properties, adhesins, and serine proteases; important for parasite motility and association with the host cell membrane. Higher in sporozoites, intermediate in tachyzoites, lower in bradyzoites.
RhoptriesConcentrate proteins associated with host cell invasion. Essential role in forming the moving junction for invasion and the parasitophorous vacuole (PV) membrane.
Dense granulesSecreted proteins released when the parasite is localized within the PV; involved in assembling a network of tubules and filamentous structures within the PV. Higher in sporozoites.

What is the Parasitophorous Vacuole (PV)?

  • A membrane-bounded compartment inside the host cell where T. gondii lives and replicates
  • The PV membrane is built largely from rhoptry proteins
  • Inside the PV, dense granule proteins build the tubule-and-filament network
Three secretory organelles, three jobs. Micronemes = motility + adhesion (get to the cell, stick to it). Rhoptries = invasion + PV (get inside, build the home). Dense granules = post-invasion remodeling (decorate the home).

T. gondii Life Cycle: Stages, Sexual vs Asexual, Cysts vs Oocysts HIGH YIELD

T. gondii uses these three stages to survive, multiply, and spread throughout its life cycle.

Definitive Host vs Intermediate Host HIGH YIELD

  • Definitive host = where sexual reproduction occurs → FELINES (cats and other felids) ONLY
  • Intermediate host = where asexual reproduction occurs → almost any mammal or bird — including humans, sheep, pigs, deer, mice, etc.

Sexual Reproduction in the Cat (the Definitive Host)

The full felid life cycle, step by step:

  1. A. Cat ingests prey containing tissue cysts (e.g., eats an infected mouse)
  2. B. The cyst wall is digested in the stomach and intestines, liberating bradyzoites (slow form)
  3. C. Bradyzoites invade the epithelial cells of the intestine (enterocytes)
  4. D. Inside enterocytes, bradyzoites divide by schizogony (a type of multiple fission), giving rise to merozoites
  5. E. Merozoites differentiate into microgamonts (male gametes) or macrogametes (female gametes)
  6. F. Fertilization gives rise to an unsporulated oocyst, which is excreted in cat feces
  7. G. Sporulation occurs in the environment over 1–5 days, generating two sporocysts, each containing four sporozoites (8 sporozoites total per sporulated oocyst)

Asexual Reproduction in Intermediate Hosts (animals, including humans)

  1. Animal consumes a sporulated oocyst (from cat feces, water, or soil)
  2. Sporozoites are released and transform into tachyzoites shortly after ingestion
  3. Tachyzoites disseminate — they go everywhere via the bloodstream and can infect almost any nucleated cell. They use micronemes/rhoptries/dense granules to invade and form a PV.
  4. Tachyzoites localize in neural and muscle tissue and develop into tissue cyst bradyzoites — the slow-growing chronic form. The cyst wall protects them indefinitely.
  5. A predator (cat) eats the infected animal → cycle restarts in the cat with the sexual phase

Key: in intermediate hosts T. gondii reproduces asexually only (tachyzoite ↔ bradyzoite). In felids it reproduces sexually (gametes → oocyst → sporozoite). Sporozoites are never produced in intermediate hosts — only in cats.

Routes of Human Infection

  • Eating raw/undercooked meat with tissue cysts (bradyzoites)
  • Ingesting sporulated oocysts from cat feces or environment
  • Transplacental transmission to a fetus when a pregnant woman is infected for the first time

Toxoplasma in Dogs

  • A lot of dogs like to eat cat litter
  • If you own both, especially with an outdoor cat, keep the litter box away from the dog
  • Toxoplasma infection of cats is generally mild or asymptomatic
  • However, dogs can develop immunosuppression, GI issues, seizures, paralysis, and sometimes death
Three stages, two reproductive modes, two host types. Tachyzoite (fast, acute, intermediate host) → Bradyzoite (slow, chronic, in tissue cysts in intermediate host) → cat eats infected meat → Sexual reproduction in cat gut (microgamont + macrogamete → oocyst) → oocyst sheds in feces, sporulates over 1–5 days → Sporozoite (in sporulated oocyst, infective again).

Mycobacterium tuberculosis: The Organism HIGH YIELD

TB Is an Ancient Disease

  • M. tuberculosis is the causative agent of TB
  • An acid-fast bacterium — NEITHER Gram-positive nor Gram-negative
  • Early humans carried M. tuberculosis with them when they migrated out of Africa ~70,000 years ago
  • 17th–18th century Europe: caused the "White Plague" — virtually everyone in Europe was infected; 25% of all deaths were from TB
  • Today: 2nd leading cause of death from infectious disease worldwide
  • Global burden: 9–10 million new active cases/year, 1.3–1.5 million deaths/year
  • Unusual age distribution: 80% of active cases are 15–49 years old (not just very young/old)
  • M. tuberculosis is an obligate pathogen — no environmental reservoir

Cell Biology Features That Allow Spread HIGH YIELD

  • Non-motile rod-shaped bacilli
  • Slow growth: doubles every 20–24 hours — this slowness:
    • Helps M. tb stay under the radar of the immune system
    • Limits effectiveness of antibiotics that target dividing bacteria (most cell-wall-active drugs only kill replicating cells)
  • Acid-fast due to long-chain mycolic acids coating its surface
    • Gives colonies a waxy appearance
    • Confers incredible chemical resistance to common sanitizers
    • Confers resistance to drying — persists in air as aerosols / on dust particles when coughed
    • Mycolic acids are unique to M. tuberculosis (and other mycobacteria); they form an extra protective outer layer

Cell Envelope Architecture (innermost → outermost)

M. tuberculosis cell wall layers: Phospholipid lipid bilayer (inner plasma membrane) ↓ Peptidoglycan (thin layer) ↓ Arabinogalactan (linker polysaccharide) ↓ Mycolic acid + outer membrane (waxy, hydrophobic, unique) ↓ Membrane proteins, lipoarabinomannan (LAM)

Why M. tb Is BSL-3 (Working With It Is Hard)

  • Most bacteria covered in this course are BSL-2 (lab coat, gloves, eye protection)
  • M. tuberculosis is BSL-3:
    • Full medical gown + scrubs
    • Specially built negative-airflow facility
    • N95 respirator or PAPR (powered air purifying respirator)
    • Sterile biosafety cabinet with constant decontamination
    • Researchers require significant training before access
  • The reason: M. tb aerosolizes, the infectious dose is low, mycolic acid resists sanitizers, and TB is serious

U.S. TB Epidemiology

  • U.S. TB cases have dropped almost every year since records were kept
  • Largely due to improvements in living conditions — sanitation, housing, and nutrition
  • However, there is a notable spike in TB in the late 1980s and early 1990s
Three structural facts to remember: (1) acid-fast (mycolic acids) — not Gram +/−, hardy in air and against sanitizers; (2) slow grower (20–24 hr doubling) — evades innate immune detection and shrugs off antibiotics that target dividing bacteria; (3) obligate pathogen (no environmental reservoir) — humans with latent TB ARE the reservoir.

TB Disease: Primary, Latent, Reactivation HIGH YIELD

The Three Disease States

StateWhat's happening
1. Primary infectionFirst exposure leads to an active infection; highly infectious — spread by persistent cough
2. LatencyM. tuberculosis remains dormant in the host inside granulomas; can persist for a lifetime
3. Reactivated infectionIf the immune system is compromised, the granuloma ruptures and a more serious infection develops — persistent cough, fever, emaciation

Spread is via airborne respiratory droplets; the infectious dose is low; crowded conditions dramatically increase transmission.

Primary Infection: Intracellular Survival in Macrophages HIGH YIELD

  1. M. tuberculosis is inhaled and reaches the alveoli
  2. It is phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages — the lung's resident sentinel phagocytes
  3. In a normal infection, the phagosome would fuse with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome that destroys the pathogen
  4. M. tuberculosis BLOCKS phagosome–lysosome fusion by releasing cell wall lipids (e.g., LAM — lipoarabinomannan) that interfere with the maturation machinery
  5. M. tuberculosis does NOT escape the phagosome — it lives in it
  6. Inside the phagosome, the bacterium is protected from other immune cells until adaptive immunity kicks in

Delay of Adaptive Immunity HIGH YIELD

Adaptive immunity normally ramps up 7–10 days after infection. In M. tuberculosis infection, adaptive immunity is delayed to 16–17 days — about 2× longer than normal.

How M. tb delays adaptive immunity:

  • M. tb infects dendritic cells (DCs) — the professional antigen-presenting cells responsible for activating naive T cells in lymph nodes
  • It impairs MHC-II antigen presentation on the DCs
  • Without proper MHC-II presentation, naive CD4+ T cells are not activated on schedule — the adaptive response is stalled
  • This buys M. tb roughly a week of extra unrestricted growth
  • Once T cells DO activate, they produce IFN-γ, which dramatically increases macrophage killing power (boosts ROS/RNS production, phagolysosome maturation), and the active infection is controlled (not eliminated)
Why the delay matters: Normal infection: Day 0 [exposure] ---------- Day 7-10 T cells activated M. tuberculosis: Day 0 [exposure] ----------------- Day 16-17 T cells activated ~7 extra days of unrestricted bacterial growth

Latency: Why TB Doesn't Just Get Cleared HIGH YIELD

  • Macrophages cannot fully kill M. tb, even when activated
  • M. tb consumes the macrophage's nutrients
  • The infected macrophage eventually dies via apoptosis
  • New macrophages arrive to clean up the apoptotic cell — and they get infected too
  • This cycle continues, recruiting more and more macrophages and other immune cells
  • The infected cells become surrounded by a halo of other immune cells — the granuloma begins to form

The Granuloma: Walling Off the Infection HIGH YIELD

DEF A granuloma is an organized cluster of immune cells that walls off the invading pathogen. The lecture's granuloma legend includes the following cell types:

  • T cell, B cell, neutrophil, NK cell
  • Macrophage, epithelioid macrophage, dendritic cell
  • Giant cell, foam cell
  • Apoptotic infected macrophage, apoptotic infected epithelioid macrophage, necrotic infected macrophage
  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis at the core

What the Granuloma Does (and Doesn't Do)

  • Does: prevents M. tb from spreading further in the body — the bacteria are essentially trapped
  • Doesn't: kill M. tb. Inside the granuloma is a continual cycle of M. tb destroying and eating macrophages — the bacteria persist for the host's lifetime
  • This is why TB is described as a chronic, contained, but not cleared infection

Reactivation

  • Due to a weakening immune system, M. tb eventually overpowers the response
  • The granuloma ruptures and a new secondary active infection begins; the cycle starts anew
  • Patients with latent infections have a ~10% lifetime reactivation risk

The Reservoir Question — Why Latency Matters Globally

  • ~1/3 of the global population (~2.7 billion people) are estimated to be latently infected with M. tb — primarily in Asia, then Africa
  • M. tuberculosis has no environmental reservoir
  • Humans with latent infections ARE the reservoir
  • This is why TB cannot be eradicated by sanitation alone — you cannot clean the soil and eliminate it. Eradicating TB requires identifying and treating latent infections in billions of people.
The TB story in five beats: (1) inhaled → alveolar macrophage, (2) blocks phagosome-lysosome fusion (mycolic acid wall lipids), (3) delays adaptive immunity 16–17 days by impairing dendritic cell MHC-II presentation, (4) granuloma forms — contained but not cleared, (5) reactivates when immunity drops (HIV is the classic trigger). The latent infection in 2.7 billion people IS the reservoir.

Common Themes

  • Both establish lifelong infection in immunocompetent hosts
  • Both can reactivate when the immune system is compromised
  • Both live in a vacuole inside the host cell rather than in the cytoplasm
  • Both have huge global prevalence in latent form

Mnemonic for the Two Systems

  • T. gondii — three stages, two reproductive modes, two host types: Tachyzoite (fast/acute) ↔ Bradyzoite (slow/cyst) in intermediate host; Sporozoite (in oocyst) only after sex in cats
  • M. tuberculosis — three disease states, three immune-evasion tricks: Primary → Latent (granuloma) → Reactivation; blocks phagolysosome fusion, delays adaptive immunity, persists in granuloma
