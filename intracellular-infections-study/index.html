The Big Picture

Lecture 22 contrasts extracellular pathogens (kill host cells from outside, replicate in the lumen) with intracellular pathogens (invade and replicate inside host cells). Two example intracellular pathogens, with very different lifestyles:

  • Listeria monocytogenes — replicates in the cytoplasm; escapes the phagosome with LLO; spreads cell-to-cell using actin rockets driven by ActA
  • Salmonella Typhi — stays inside a vacuole (SCV); resists acidification and immune killing; escapes when nutrients run out by inducing host apoptosis

Both pathogens go inside cells, but for opposite reasons: Listeria uses the cytoplasm as a free buffet of nutrients hidden from antibodies; Salmonella uses the vacuole as a hideout from host antimicrobial systems. The fork is: cytoplasm vs vacuole.

Extracellular vs Intracellular Pathogens HIGH YIELD

DEF Extracellular pathogens kill host cells from the outside and replicate outside of host cells.

  • Example: Enterohemorrhagic E. coli kills intestinal epithelial cells by secreting toxins — never invades cells; replicates in the lumen of the intestine

DEF Intracellular pathogens can invade and replicate within host cells. The ability to do this is often crucial for their pathogenesis.

  • Two examples covered in this lecture: Listeria monocytogenes and Salmonella Typhi
  • But there are many more — classified by where in the host cell they replicate

Survival Strategies of Intracellular Pathogens

Lecture flagged two strategies in particular:

  • Actin-based protrusion (L. monocytogenes, Shigella flexneri, Rickettsia) — a single bacterium uses force from actin polymerization to protrude from the cell membrane and force engulfment into a neighboring cell
  • Apoptosis (Salmonella, L. monocytogenes, Shigella) — the pathogen induces programmed cell death via virulence factors to release itself

Other strategies on the diagram: cytolysis, budding (Orientia), extrusion (Chlamydia), phagosomal expulsion (Cryptococcus), pyroptosis (sensing flagellin → lytic cell death).

The cytoplasm vs vacuole distinction matters because each location demands a different toolkit. Cytoplasm-dwellers need to break out of the phagosome first; vacuole-dwellers need to resist what the phagosome would normally do (acidify, fuse with lysosomes).

Listeria monocytogenes: Entry, LLO, ActA HIGH YIELD

L. monocytogenes Basics

  • Gram-positive bacillus (rod)
  • Usually found in the soil; eats decaying matter (a saprophyte)
  • Infects diverse organisms: protists, arthropods, mammals
  • Named after Joseph Lister, the microbiologist who pioneered sterility in surgery
  • "monocytogenes" = the dramatic accumulation of monocytes seen during infection
  • Humans most often exposed via contaminated food (processed meat, milk products)
  • Tolerates cold, high salt, and low moisture — survives refrigeration!

Disease: How Listeria Makes Us Sick

  • Ingested → colonizes digestive tract
  • In healthy people: shed without incident (maybe mild diarrhea)
  • In immunocompromised people: crosses the intestinal barrier, travels through the lymphatic system, gains access to other tissues. Symptoms develop 3–4 weeks after exposure
  • If it enters the bloodstream → septicemia
  • If it crosses the blood–brain barrier → meningitis and encephalitis
  • If ingested during pregnancy → can cross the placental barrier → miscarriage

Step 1 — Host Cell Entry: Internalins A & B HIGH YIELD

  • Listeria displays two surface proteins, Internalin A and Internalin B (InlA, InlB)
  • The internalins bind a receptor on the surface of the epithelial cell
  • This triggers a signal transduction cascade that reorganizes the host's cytoskeleton, causing the membrane to ruffle
  • The ruffling allows phagocytosis of Listeria — important because epithelial cells are NOT normally phagocytic
  • This is sometimes called the "zipper" mechanism — the cell membrane wraps closely around the bacterium during uptake
  • Once inside, instead of being trafficked to the lysosome, Listeria escapes the phagosome and enters the cytoplasm
Step 1: Internalin-driven entry (the "zipper") InlA / InlB on Listeria → receptor on epithelial cell ↓ signal transduction cascade ↓ cytoskeleton reorganization ↓ membrane ruffling → phagocytosis

Step 2 — Phagosome Escape: Listeriolysin O (LLO) HIGH YIELD

  • Once engulfed, Listeria secretes listeriolysin O (LLO), a pore-forming toxin
  • LLO is assembled from dozens of monomers
  • It assembles only in acidic pH — this is critical: the phagosome acidifies, but the cytoplasm is neutral, so LLO won't accidentally punch holes in the host cell after escape
  • Each monomer binds cholesterol-rich membranes of the phagosome (a "cholesterol-dependent cytolysin")
  • Linear oligomers form, then drive themselves into the membrane to create pores
  • Ions pass freely → the phagosome ruptures → Listeria released into the cytoplasm

LLO belongs to a wider family — many bacteria use cholesterol-dependent cytolysins:

SpeciesToxinEffect
Bacillus anthracisAnthrolysin ODisrupts epithelial barriers
Clostridium perfringensPerfringolysin OInhibits leukocyte migration
Listeria monocytogenesListeriolysin OLyses phagosome
Streptococcus pneumoniaePneumolysinInduces inflammation and apoptosis
Streptococcus pyogenesStreptolysin OApoptosis of phagocytes & epithelial cells

Step 3 — Cytoplasmic Replication

  • Once liberated, Listeria multiplies in the cytoplasm
  • The cytoplasm is a buffet: bacteria scavenge host nutrients freely
  • And, critically, they are protected from phagocytes, complement, and antibodies — all of which act in the extracellular space
  • But one host cell only has so many nutrients

Step 4 — Cell-to-Cell Spread: ActA & Actin Rockets HIGH YIELD

  • To keep replicating, Listeria hijacks host machinery to move into a new cell instead of leaving the cytoplasm
  • It manipulates host actin and induces it to polymerize at one pole, propelling the bacterium forward as actin filaments assemble behind it

The ActA Mechanism (step by step)

  1. Listeria displays the protein ActA on its surface at one pole (asymmetric)
  2. ActA stimulates host actin polymerization at that pole
  3. Short actin filaments form at the ActA pole
  4. As the bacillus jiggles in Brownian motion, gaps behind it are immediately stuffed with actin filaments — this drives the bacterium forward
  5. At random, some bacteria are propelled out of the cell into a neighboring cell
  6. In the new cell, Listeria is wrapped in a double membrane (one from each cell) that it uses LLO to break out of (LLO appears twice in the lifecycle!)
Cell-to-cell spread (actin rocket model): Cell A: Cell B: [Listeria]==> [Listeria in actin tail double membrane] ↓ LLO escape ↓ new cytoplasmic cycle

Why this matters: spread by actin rockets means Listeria never enters the extracellular space. So antibodies, complement, and most phagocytes never get a shot at it. This is how Listeria spreads to many body sites (gut → liver → CNS → placenta).

Three-protein Listeria story: InlA/InlB trick the epithelial cell into eating the bacterium → LLO (acidic-pH activated, cholesterol-dependent) lyses the phagosome → ActA co-opts host actin to make a "rocket tail" that drives cell-to-cell spread without exposing Listeria to the extracellular immune system.

Salmonella: Disease, Entry, SCV Survival, Escape HIGH YIELD

Salmonella Basics

  • Gram-negative, rod-shaped, mostly motile, non-spore-forming
  • Often have peritrichous flagella (covered in flagella all over)
  • Facultative anaerobe
  • Two species: S. bongori and S. enterica; S. enterica has 6 subspecies
  • S. enterica subsp. enterica is responsible for ~99% of human disease
  • Further classified by serotype (using antigens) — over 2,500 serotypes
  • Typhi is one such serotype: full name Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serotype Typhi — usually shortened to Salmonella Typhi (note: Typhi is roman, not italic)

Disease Manifestations HIGH YIELD

Salmonella can cause four distinct disease manifestations in humans:

ManifestationBurden
1. Enteric fever (typhoid)11–21 million cases/year, ~200K deaths
2. Gastroenteritis (diarrhea)200 million–1.3 billion cases/year, up to 3 million deaths
3. Bacteremia268K deaths worldwide in 2015
4. Chronic asymptomatic carriage2–5% of typhoid survivors; reservoir for outbreaks

Typhoid Fever

  • Begins with ingestion of a S. Typhi strain — usually from contaminated water, animal products, or close contact with an infected person
  • Symptoms begin 1–2 weeks after exposure
  • Generalized fever, malaise, abdominal pain ± headache, myalgias, nausea, anorexia, constipation — sometimes diarrhea
  • Fever begins mild and progressively worsens (up to 104°F)
  • If untreated, patients can enter the "typhoid state": delirious, lying motionless with eyes half open
  • Mortality (typhoid state, untreated): 10–30%
  • Mortality (typhoid state, treated): 1–4%
  • Mortality (treated earlier): 0.2%

Chronic Asymptomatic Carriage

  • 2–5% of typhoid survivors don't fully clear the infection — they become asymptomatic carriers
  • Three things must happen: (1) successful breach of the intestinal epithelial barrier; (2) evasion of early innate immune killing; (3) localization to a permissive niche — the biliary tract and gallbladder (often forming biofilms on gallstones)
  • Carriers intermittently shed the bacteria for prolonged, ill-defined periods — spreading the disease and maintaining a reservoir of infection
  • Most famous chronic asymptomatic carrier: Typhoid Mary

Mode of Entry: How Salmonella Crosses the Gut Epithelium HIGH YIELD

The mode of entry depends on the cell type Salmonella encounters in the gut epithelium. Three routes:

Cell typeEntry mechanism
M cells (specialized epithelial cells of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue that present antigens to immune cells)Receptor-mediated endocytosis
Dendritic cellsPhagocytosis
Epithelial cellsMembranes are modified by SPI1 (Salmonella Pathogenicity Island 1) to force entry — SPI1 induces membrane ruffling and cytoskeletal rearrangements

Once across the epithelium and inside the gut lumen tissues, Salmonella is taken up by macrophages, T cells, B cells, neutrophils and other immune cells — rather than being destroyed there, it survives and uses these as travel vehicles.

Survival Inside Cells: The Salmonella-Containing Vacuole (SCV) HIGH YIELD

DEF The Salmonella-Containing Vacuole (SCV) is the membrane-bound compartment Salmonella establishes inside host cells — unlike Listeria, Salmonella stays inside a vacuole rather than escaping to the cytoplasm.

  • The SCV acidifies — pH drops from 6.5 to 5.5
  • Salmonella has virulence factors that resist acidification (encoded by SPI2 and others)

Other virulence factors that maintain the SCV niche:

  • Degrade lysosomes — prevent fusion of the SCV with lysosomes that would deliver hydrolases
  • Block nitrosative and oxidative stress (e.g., nirC, arginase, Hmp; katE, katG, sod, rpoS, slyA) — counters the macrophage's reactive nitrogen and oxygen species
  • Impair inflammation (sipB, AvrA, SspH1)
  • Block host antimicrobial peptides (sapABCDF, yejABEF)

Escape from the Host Cell HIGH YIELD

  • Salmonella lives protected within SCVs until the cell's nutrients are depleted
  • When nutrients run out, Salmonella induces macrophage programmed death (apoptosis) via SPI1 effectors
  • The dying macrophage releases the bacteria — they search for a new cell to infect
Salmonella intracellular life-cycle: Entry → SCV forms (pH 6.5) ↓ acidifies (pH 5.5) ↓ resist acid + block ROS/RNS + block lysosome ↓ replicate inside SCV ↓ nutrients depleted → induce apoptosis ↓ released → new host cell

Drug Resistance — A Growing Threat

  • Drug-resistant nontyphoidal Salmonella: ~212,500 infections/year, ~70 deaths/year — CDC threat level SERIOUS
  • Drug-resistant Salmonella serotype Typhi: ~4,100 infections/year, <5 deaths/year — CDC threat level SERIOUS (typhoid fever can lead to bowel rupture, shock, and death)
The Salmonella story has three pillars: cell-type-specific entry (M cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis, dendritic cells via phagocytosis, epithelial cells via SPI1-induced ruffling), survival in the SCV (resists acidification, blocks lysosome fusion, neutralizes ROS/RNS), and nutrient-triggered escape (induced macrophage apoptosis). Compare to Listeria: cytoplasm vs vacuole, actin rockets vs apoptosis-mediated escape.

Typhoid Mary: The First Documented Healthy Carrier HIGH YIELD

Mary Mallon, 1869–1938

  • Mary Mallon moved to the US from Ireland in 1883 at age 14
  • Literate but had no family; struggled to survive
  • Eventually became a cook to wealthy families in New York

The Investigation

  • In 1906, 6 of 11 people in one household where Mary worked got typhoid fever
  • Engineer George Soper initially blamed freshwater clams — but realized that not everyone who ate the clams got sick, and turned his suspicion to Mary
  • Soper checked her work history: 7 of 8 families she had worked for had typhoid cases — 22 people infected, some died
  • In 1907, Soper published the first paper describing a "healthy carrier" of S. Typhi in the US
  • 1907 also saw 3,000 New Yorkers infected with typhoid — likely linked to Mary

First Quarantine (1907–1910)

  • Mary ran from authorities, but was caught and forced to give a stool sample — positive for S. Typhi
  • Quarantined for 2 years at Riverside Hospital in a cottage on North Brother Island
  • Released in 1910 with the order: never cook again

Return to Cooking

  • Other jobs didn't pay enough; she got injured and couldn't work
  • Used the fake name "Mary Brown" to get cooking jobs at multiple places — changed jobs faster than Soper could find her
  • Eventually got a job as a cook at Sloane Maternity in Manhattan
  • Within 3 months, 25 people were infected and 2 died
  • When she was found out, nearly all the places she had worked had Salmonella outbreaks

Second Quarantine (1915–1938)

  • Re-quarantined on North Brother Island in 1915
  • There until she died in 1938
  • Many treatments were tested on her in quarantine — none worked (she likely had a gallbladder biofilm reservoir)
  • In total, Mary directly infected at least 122 people and caused at least 5 deaths
  • Officials later identified over 400 other asymptomatic carriers in New York — none of them received the same treatment as Mary

Why Mary Could Be a Lifelong Carrier (the biology)

Chronic asymptomatic carriage of Salmonella requires:

  1. Successful breach of the intestinal epithelial barrier
  2. Evasion of early innate immune-mediated killing
  3. Localization to a permissive niche — the biliary tract and gallbladder, where S. Typhi forms biofilms on gallstones

Carriers shed the bacteria intermittently and unpredictably — which is exactly why a cook with no symptoms could repeatedly seed outbreaks and why treatments tried on Mary failed (the gallbladder biofilm is hard to clear).

Typhoid Mary is the textbook case for chronic asymptomatic carriage: it shows that intracellular pathogens can establish lifelong, symptom-free reservoirs (biofilms on gallstones) and that public health responses to identical biology have always been shaped by class, gender, and prejudice — ~400 other carriers were never quarantined.

Common Themes

  • Both are food/water-borne intracellular pathogens that breach the gut epithelium
  • Both have cell-type-specific entry mechanisms involving cytoskeletal rearrangement
  • Both are far more dangerous in immunocompromised hosts
  • Both pathogens use long-distance dissemination through the body to reach severe sites (CNS for Listeria, gallbladder/blood for Salmonella)

The Cytoplasm vs Vacuole Mnemonic

  • Listeria = "Liberty" — lyses out of the phagosome (LLO) and lives FREE in the cytoplasm; spreads via actin rockets (ActA)
  • Salmonella = "Stays in" — Stays in the vacuole (SCV), Survives by modifying it, eScapes by inducing apoptosis

Three-Letter Memory for Each Pathogen

  • Listeria: InlA/InlB (entry) → LLO (escape phagosome) → ActA (rocket spread)
  • Salmonella: SPI1 (entry) → SCV (vacuole survival) → apoptosis induction (escape when nutrients run out)
