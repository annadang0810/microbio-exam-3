Why Microbial Ecology is Hard

The aim of all ecology is to determine which organisms are present in an environment and what they are doing there. For microbes, both questions are difficult because microbe morphology reveals very little about identity. We have to use genome sequence and metabolism instead.

Themes of Microbial Ecology

  • Ubiquity — microbes are present everywhere on Earth where liquid water exists
  • Abundance — microbes occur in huge numbers (~1030 bacteria + archaea on Earth; 1 L seawater = ~1 billion bacteria from 20,000+ species)
  • Metabolic Power — extremely chemically active in diverse ways; squeeze every bit of energy from chemicals
  • Communities — microbes live in groups that allow them to perform functions individual cells never could

The Two Approaches

ApproachWhat it does
Cultivation-DEPENDENTGrow microbes in the lab to study them — oldest method
Cultivation-INDEPENDENTAssess microbial communities directly in the sample through sequencing — bypasses the need to grow them
Both approaches are needed. Cultivation gives you organisms to do experiments on. Cultivation-independent methods give you the true diversity of who is there — including the >99% we cannot grow.

Syntrophy HIGH YIELD

DEF Syntrophy = an interaction where microbes feed together: one microbe catalyzes a reaction, and another eats the reaction product. Microbes exchange metabolic byproducts needed for growth.

Why Syntrophy Exists

  • One microbe depends on its neighbor to consume waste products that would otherwise accumulate and inhibit its own metabolism (feedback inhibition)
  • The neighbor benefits because the waste is its food source
  • This is the basis of interdependent nutritional consortia — one of the main reasons many microbes flourish in nature but cannot be grown alone in pure culture

Example 1 — Anaerobic Hydrocarbon Degradation

  • Anaerobic degradation of hydrocarbons generates butyrate
  • Other bacteria use butyrate as a carbon source
  • If butyrate were to accumulate, hydrocarbon degradation would stop (product feedback inhibition)
  • If you tried to cultivate the hydrocarbon degrader alone, you would have to manually remove butyrate from the system — in nature, the partner does it for free

Example 2 — Methanobacillus omelianskii (the lecture's classic example)

Originally thought to be a single species that grew in pure culture by converting ethanol + CO2 to methane.

Actually it's TWO organisms:

  • The S-organism — a bacterium
  • Methanobacterium bryantii — an archaeon

The S-organism Reaction (only works if H2 is removed)

S-organism (bacterium): ethanol → acetate + H2 but only if H2 is removed! M. bryantii (archaeon): H2 + CO2 → methane (CH4) Together (the "fake species"): ethanol + CO2 → acetate + methane

The S-organism's H2 is thermodynamically inhibitory — if it builds up, the ethanol-fermentation reaction stops being favorable. The archaeon constantly siphons off the H2 by making methane, which keeps the bacterium's reaction running. Neither can grow alone; together they look like a single organism.

Why this matters for cultivation: when you try to grow microbes from nature alone, you may have broken the syntrophic partnership they need. This is one of the main reasons most microbes are unculturable — they're missing their "lab partner."

Cultivation-Dependent vs Cultivation-Independent Methods HIGH YIELD

Cultivation-DEPENDENT Methods

The oldest method of identification. Mimic the microbe's natural environment so that you can grow it in the lab. Variables you change to favor your target organism:

  • Different food sources — e.g., for a bacterium that infects plants, grow with cellulose
  • Different atmospheric conditions — anaerobic, aerobic, microaerobic, etc.
  • Different temperatures
  • Different pH

Even with the most valiant effort, only a small fraction of microbes can be recovered by culture.

Enrichment Culture

DEF Enrichment = adjusting culture conditions to favor the growth of the desired microbe(s). It is a crucial step for cultivation.

Lecture example — enriching for anaerobic photosynthesizers in phylum Chloroflexi:

  • Add a small amount of soil to an oxygen-free medium supplemented with sulfur compounds
  • Fill the head space of the culture vessel with CO2 gas
  • Incubate in the presence of light

Why Cultivation Is Still Important

  • Genomes only give you the code — not how it works under different conditions
  • Cultivation is essential for hypothesis testing
  • The presence of a gene does NOT mean it is being used when and how you think
  • You need controlled genetic experiments to prove a gene's function — and cultivation is a prerequisite for that

Cultivation-INDEPENDENT Methods

Answer the simple question of WHO is there by skipping cultivation entirely. These methods indirectly assess the abundance and taxonomic affiliation of microbes in environmental samples by sequencing or hybridizing nucleic acids directly from the sample.

The three main cultivation-independent methods:

  • Amplicon-based community profiling (e.g., 16S rRNA sequencing)
  • Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
  • Shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Side-by-Side

Cultivation-DependentCultivation-Independent
What you doGrow the microbes in the labSequence/hybridize DNA from the sample
StrengthLets you do experiments & test hypothesesCaptures unculturable diversity (the >99%)
WeaknessStrong cultivation bias; misses most microbesTells you who but not always what they're doing in real time
ExamplesEnrichment cultures, selective media16S amplicon, FISH, shotgun metagenomics
The two are complementary, not competing. Cultivation-independent methods tell you who lives in the community; cultivation lets you take individual microbes back to the bench and figure out what they actually do.

Cultivation Bias HIGH YIELD

DEF Cultivation bias = the systematic distortion in our knowledge of microbes that arises because we only cultivate microbes that grow well in our chosen culturing condition. The microbes we know best are not the most ecologically important — they're just the easiest to grow.

Why It Happens

  • You can only cultivate microbes that grow best in your particular culture conditions
  • You don't even know what the optimal conditions are for most microbes — how could you, if you've never seen them grow?
  • Only a handful of organisms grow well in the most common culture medium/condition: Luria Broth (LB) in ambient air (aerobic)

Why We Know E. coli Best

  • E. coli is an aerobic chemoheterotroph — grows well on LB in ambient air
  • "They are easy to culture!" (lecture quote)
  • We're also motivated to study E. coli-like organisms because many KNOWN pathogenic microbes fall in this class — aerobic chemoheterotrophs

What Gets Missed

  • Anaerobes — can't grow on aerobic LB; need oxygen-free conditions
  • Autotrophs — don't eat the carbon sources we provide; fix CO2 instead
  • These groups have a larger ecological impact than the cultured aerobic chemoheterotrophs we focus on, but we know far less about them
  • Microbes that depend on syntrophy — can't be cultivated alone because they need a partner

The Practical Consequence

Our textbook and database picture of "what microbes do" is heavily skewed toward a small slice of microbial diversity. This is a major reason cultivation-independent methods (amplicon, FISH, shotgun) revolutionized microbial ecology — they finally let us see what we'd been missing.

One sentence summary: cultivation bias means we know the easiest microbes, not the most important microbes — aerobic chemoheterotrophs like E. coli, which happen to include many human pathogens, while ecologically critical anaerobes and autotrophs remain understudied.

Amplicon-Based Community Profiling HIGH YIELD

A commonly used culture-independent method that detects microbe-specific marker genes from environmental samples.

Target Genes

  • 16S rRNA — for prokaryotes (Bacteria + Archaea)
  • 18S rRNA — for eukaryotes

Workflow

1. Extract DNA from environmental sample 2. Amplify target sequences (16S rRNA) using PCR 3. Clone into bacteria OR sequence directly 4. Compare reads against gene databases → identify homologs 5. Build phylogenetic trees → infer relatedness 6. Group into OTUs (operational taxonomic units)

Why 16S/18S rRNA? (3 must-have features)

  • ALL microbes within the community must have this gene — otherwise you'd miss them
  • It must be possible to design UNIVERSAL PCR primers that anneal to the gene in all microbes (so you only need one primer set, not one per species)
  • There must also be VARIABLE regions of this gene that are different in all microbes (so you can tell them apart)

16S and 18S rRNA fit the bill: highly conserved (universal primers work) but with variable regions (species discrimination).

Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs)

Sequences are lumped into OTUs based on sequence similarity. Specific homology thresholds are used to classify OTUs at each taxonomic level:

Taxonomic levelSequence identity threshold
Species98.7%
Genus94.5%
Family86.5%

The output reveals the relative abundance of each OTU in the native community.

Limitations of Amplicon Sequencing

  • The "universal" primers are not actually completely universal — they don't equally amplify the target gene in all species
  • Universal primers are designed from sequences already in databases — they don't capture the diversity of organisms we haven't seen yet
  • Least abundant microbes are under-sampled and missed because PCR amplification is exponential (rare templates lose out to common ones)
  • Can usually only reliably give genus-level resolution
  • Tells you only WHO is there — no genomic or functional information
Amplicon = "WHO is there." It's fast and cheap, but biased and tells you nothing about what they're doing.

Fluorescence in situ Hybridization (FISH) HIGH YIELD

FISH uses fluorescent DNA probes that hybridize directly to DNA/RNA in intact cells in a sample. Unlike amplicon sequencing, FISH does not destroy the sample — you can see the cells in their actual location.

How It Works

  • The same PCR primers used for amplicon sequencing can be used to make fluorescent DNA probes
  • Probes target the 16S rRNA in prokaryotes (the rRNA inside intact cells)
  • Both universal probes (label all bacteria) and genus-specific probes (label one specific genus) can be designed
  • Probes are hybridized with the sample and visualized with a fluorescent microscope

Example: Bacteria vs Archaea

FISH probes that distinguish between the 16S rRNA gene of Bacteria and Archaea can be used to tag members of the two domains in the same sample — one color for Bacteria, another for Archaea — and visualize their relative locations.

Major Advantage: SPATIAL INFORMATION

  • FISH preserves the 3D architecture of the microbial community — you see who is next to whom
  • Used to understand the architecture of the oral microbiome — we can see how bacteria spatially arrange within dental plaque
  • Each color represents a different genus, so you can map out the community structure

Limitations

  • Limited by how many colors you can distinguish simultaneously in a microscope — usually a handful
  • You have to already know what to look for to design the probes — not a discovery method on its own
  • Lower throughput than sequencing-based methods
FISH = "WHERE in space." It's the only method (among amplicon, FISH, shotgun) that preserves spatial architecture — useful for biofilms and tissue-associated communities like the oral microbiome.

Shotgun Metagenomic Sequencing HIGH YIELD

An innovation in sequencing technology made shotgun metagenomics possible. The method sequences ALL the DNA present within the sample — not just one marker gene.

What It Captures

  • At sufficient sequencing depth, the entire genome of every microbe in the sample can theoretically be sequenced
  • Eliminates amplicon bias — no PCR primers, no preferential amplification
  • Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryotes, and viruses can all be detected in a single sequencing run
  • Tells you the WHO, WHAT, and HOW of microbial ecology — identity AND function

The Trade-off

  • More expensive than amplicon sequencing
  • Requires more sample DNA and deeper sequencing to get full coverage
  • Computationally intensive analysis

Workflow

1. Total genomic DNA extracted from sample 2. DNA sheared into small fragments for Illumina sequencing 3. Illumina deep sequencing (millions–billions of reads) 4. Taxonomic classification of reads via marker gene analysis → gives relative abundance info (like amplicon, but unbiased) 5. Assembly-based analysis to construct MAGs = metagenome associated genomes 6. MAG annotation & functional profiling: - antibiotic resistance genes - metabolic pathways - virulence genes - etc.

MAGs: Metagenome-Associated Genomes

By assembling shotgun reads, researchers can reconstruct draft genomes for individual microbes in the community — even ones that have never been cultured. This has revealed huge swaths of microbial diversity and revolutionized our understanding of the unculturable majority.

Shotgun = "WHO + WHAT + HOW." It tells you who is there, what genes they have, and what metabolic / virulence / resistance functions they're capable of. The most powerful method — and the most expensive.

Quick Picking Guide

  • Want a quick, cheap census of who's there? → Amplicon
  • Want to see how cells are arranged in 3D space (e.g., a biofilm)? → FISH
  • Want unbiased identity + functional capability + viruses + everything? → Shotgun

Mnemonics

  • Amplicon = "Ask who" (cheap, narrow, 16S only)
  • FISH = "Find in situ" (spatial, microscopy)
  • Shotgun = "See everything" (all DNA, all domains, all functions)
Don't confuse them: amplicon and shotgun both end up as sequencing reads, but amplicon only sequences ONE gene (after PCR amplifying it) while shotgun sequences EVERYTHING (no PCR). FISH doesn't sequence at all — it visualizes.
