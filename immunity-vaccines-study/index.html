Two Branches of Immunity

Despite living in a world teeming with microbes, we are rarely infected — thanks to our immune system. It has two main branches that work together.

FeatureInnate ImmunityAdaptive Immunity
AvailabilityAlways available (constitutive)Set in motion by contact with a particular agent
SpeedFast (minutes–hours)Slow at first (days–weeks)
SpecificityBroad (recognizes MAMPs)Highly specific (recognizes individual antigens)
MemoryNoneYES — stronger response on re-exposure
ComponentsSkin, mucous membranes, AMPs, lysozyme, complement, phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells)B cells (humoral / antibodies), T cells (cell-mediated)

The Cascade: Innate → Adaptive

  • Innate starts with the skin, then resident immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells)
  • When trouble is sensed, they call for backup → circulating cells (neutrophils) join the fight
  • If infection is bad, dendritic cells activate the adaptive response
  • T-cell activation → more T-cells + activation of B-cells
  • B and T cells then enhance the innate response to clear the infection

Three Factors That Decide Disease Outcome

  1. Inoculum size (number of invading microbes)
  2. Virulence (invasive ability of the agent)
  3. Strength of host defenses

If virulence is high (e.g. Shigella), required inoculum is low. If host defenses are strong, even a high inoculum may be cleared.

Think of it like a relay race: skin/mucous → complement → phagocytes → dendritic cells present antigen → T cells activate → B cells make antibodies. Each step buys time and amplifies the next.

Innate Immunity: Barriers to Entry

1. Mechanical Barrier — Skin

Like a massive wall physically keeping microbes out.

2. Antimicrobial Substances

  • Skin: high salt, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and fatty acids (made by normal skin bacteria)
  • Mucous membranes (respiratory, digestive, genitourinary): antibodies, lysozyme, AMPs
  • Stomach acid (low pH): kills most ingested microbes
  • Bile salts in intestine
  • Complement in circulation (major factor)

3. Physical Currents That Sweep Microbes Away

  • Respiratory cilia — ciliated epithelial cells beat mucus + trapped microbes upward (mucociliary escalator)
  • Peristalsis — intestinal contractions move microbes through the GI tract
  • Urinary flow — flushes microbes out of the urinary tract

What Triggers the Innate Response?

Pattern-recognition receptors detect MAMPs (microbe-associated molecular patterns) — conserved microbial features that are essential for fitness, so microbes can't easily evolve away from them.

Common MAMPs

  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — gram-negative outer membrane
  • Peptidoglycan — bacterial cell wall
  • Flagellin — flagellum protein
  • Pilin — pilus protein

The human innate immune system can recognize ~1,000 distinct MAMPs. How we avoid constant inflammation despite our microbiota is still not fully understood.

Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)

ReceptorLocationWhat It Does
TLR (Toll-like receptor)Cell surface, facing outwardBinds extracellular MAMPs → activates NF-κB → cytokine genes + cell survival
NLR (NOD-like receptor)CytoplasmDetects MAMPs that breached the phagosome → can do 3 things: NF-κB activation, inflammasome assembly → pyroptosis, neutrophil chemotaxis
Mnemonic: TLRs face OUT (look at extracellular world). NLRs face IN (look at cytoplasm). Both signal through NF-κB to "sound the alarm."

Inflammation

The host's most powerful reaction to an invading agent. Characterized by reddening, swelling, pain, and pus.

The Four Cardinal Signs

SignCause
ReddeningIncreased blood flow to the area
SwellingTissues engorged with fluid containing immune effectors
PainRelease of chemical mediators + compression of nerves
PusDead white blood cells

Recruitment of WBCs

  1. Complement activation generates chemotaxins at the infection site
  2. WBCs in the bloodstream squeeze through the blood vessel walls — this is diapedesis
  3. WBCs move up the chemotactic gradient to the infection
  4. WBCs encounter bacteria and phagocytose them

Disease = Damage to Host Tissue

Damage can come from:

  • The microbe directly (e.g. toxins)
  • The host cell triggering its own death
  • The host's own immune response via inflammation — often the biggest source of damage!

Examples of Host Damage

Phagocytes — The Innate Killers

Phagocytes are cells with the innate ability to engulf foreign cells. Phagocytosis is one of the most effective innate defenses — the majority of bacteria invading humans are killed this way.

Steps in Phagocytosis

  1. Pseudopods engulf the microbe → trapped in a vesicle called the phagosome
  2. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome → forms the phagolysosome
  3. Microbe is digested by hydrolytic enzymes + oxidative enzymes + AMPs in the phagolysosome

Neutrophils — First Responders

  • Most numerous phagocytes
  • Short-lived (~1 week), cannot proliferate (terminally differentiated)
  • On a "seek and destroy" mission
  • Loaded with lysosomal granules + oxidative enzymes that make hypochlorite (bleach!)
  • Recruited by chemotaxins (complement-derived + bacterial products)
  • Require opsonins to be effective
  • Can also form NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps) — choose to die and release DNA + antimicrobials to entrap pathogens

Macrophages & Monocytes — The Coordinators

  • Monocytes = long-lived; circulate in blood; can differentiate further
  • Macrophages = monocytes that have entered tissues
  • Arrive at the site of infection more slowly than neutrophils
  • Act as "garbage collectors" — clean up microbial debris and remaining microbes after the neutrophil-microbe battle
  • Activated by cytokines
  • Produce cytokines that attract more neutrophils
  • Help initiate cell-mediated (adaptive) immunity by presenting antigen to T cells

Dendritic Cells — The Bridge

  • Specialized for antigen presentation
  • Sample antigens at infection sites, then migrate to lymph nodes
  • Activate T cells — this is the spark that lights the adaptive immune response

The Complement System

A soluble (not cell-based) defense system in the blood made of ~30 different proteins. Triggered by MAMPs and antibodies.

Three Activation Pathways

PathwayTrigger
ClassicalAntigen-antibody complex (links to adaptive immunity!)
LectinPathogens bound by host lectin (mannose-binding protein)
AlternativeSurface of pathogen directly (no antibody needed)

All three pathways converge on C3 convertase, which sets off a sequential proteolytic cascade. The cleavage products are pharmacologically active peptides that coordinate the inflammatory response.

The 3 Outputs of Complement

  1. Inflammation & phagocyte recruitment — C3a, C4a, C5a are mediators of inflammation and chemotaxins
  2. Opsonization — C3b coats the pathogen to make it palatable for phagocytes
  3. Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) — C5b + C6, 7, 8, 9 assemble pores in foreign membranes → lyses prokaryotic cells, eukaryotic cells, and even enveloped viruses

Opsonization

  • At rest, phagocytes are "sluggish eaters"
  • Opsonins (C3b) coat invading bacteria, making them more palatable
  • Antibodies (from the adaptive response) can also act as opsonins
  • Opsonin receptors on the phagocyte interact with the coating in a zipper-like fashion until the prey is engulfed

Avoiding Self-Attack

Host cells display sialic acid on their surfaces — this is the "self" tag that prevents complement from attacking host membranes. Without it, MAC would attack host cells (auto-immunity).

Mnemonic: Complement does 3 things — "I.O.M." — Inflammation, Opsonization, MAC. C3a/C5a recruit phagocytes; C3b coats them for eating; C5b-C9 punches a hole.

T-Cells & the Cell-Mediated Response HIGH YIELD

T cells are responsible for the cell-mediated immune response (CMIR). They eliminate intracellular microbes that survive within phagocytes or other infected cells.

Cell-Mediated Immune Response (3 steps)

  1. Mediated by T-cells — the TCR recognizes antigen bound to MHC
  2. When non-self antigen is detected, the T-cell is activated to kill infected cells
  3. Activated T-cells are also selected for clonal expansion (more T-cells made)

The Two T-Cell Types

TypeFunction
T helper lymphocytesPromote differentiation of B lymphocytes; activate macrophages
Cytotoxic T lymphocytesKill antigen-presenting cells (i.e., infected cells displaying foreign antigen)

Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC)

  • Class I MHC molecules are found on ALL types of nucleated cells
  • Class II MHC molecules are found only on antigen presenting cells
  • MHC II is required for T-cell communication with macrophages, dendritic cells, and B-cells

MHC and Antigen Processing

PathwayWhat it does
Endogenous antigen processingClass I MHC binds antigens that originate in the cytoplasm (inside) of the cell and presents them to T-cells
Exogenous antigen processingClass II MHC binds antigens that originate from outside the cell and presents them to T-cells

T-Cell Education: Positive & Negative Selection

  • Positive selection: enriches for T-cells that are functional — bind self-MHC molecules with good affinity. Failures (low affinity) are destroyed by apoptosis.
  • Negative selection: of T-cells that survive positive selection, eliminates those that would attack the body's own cells — bind self-MHC with too high affinity. Prompts apoptosis → tolerance.

Result: surviving T-cells have good (appropriate) affinity for self-MHC — they can present antigens but don't attack self-tissues.

T-Cells Enhance Innate Immunity

T-cells can activate macrophages through the production of IFN-γ. Activated macrophages have:

  • Increased metabolism & lysosomal proteins
  • Production of cytokines & growth factors
  • Increased phagocytosis
Two T-cell jobs: helper (activates B cells & macrophages) and cytotoxic (kills infected antigen-presenting cells). Both must first be educated through positive (recognize self-MHC) and negative (don't attack self) selection.

B-Cells & the Humoral Response HIGH YIELD

B cells are responsible for the humoral immune response (HIR), mediated by antibodies. They eliminate extracellular microbes and their toxins.

Humoral Immune Response (3 steps)

  1. B-cells recognize specific antigens → proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells
  2. Antibodies bind to specific antigens on microbes
  3. Some B-cells evolve into memory cells

Plasma Cells

  • Differentiated B cells specialized for antibody secretion
  • All antibodies from one plasma cell are identical (same specificity)

Memory B Cells

  • Form during the primary response and remain after infection clears
  • On re-exposure to the same antigen, they activate within hours
  • This is the cellular basis for the secondary humoral response and for vaccines
B cells → plasma cells (antibody factories) + memory cells (insurance for next exposure). Memory cells are why vaccines work: they let your body skip the slow primary response and go straight to the fast secondary response.

Antibodies & The Antibody Response HIGH YIELD

What Is an Antibody?

  • Also called immunoglobulins (Ig)
  • Proteins secreted by plasma cells
  • Have a variable region capable of binding specifically with antigen detected by B cells
  • Grouped into one of five Ig classes determined by the constant region: IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, IgE

Primary Humoral Immune Response (first exposure)

  • Cell proliferation & differentiation upon first antigen exposure
  • Lag period: 3 to 6 days
  • Peak antibody levels reached in 10 days
  • Antibody levels then decline

Secondary Humoral Immune Response (re-exposure)

  • Re-exposure to the same antigen gives a faster, more prolonged, more effective response
  • Sensitized memory cells respond within hours
  • Antibody levels peak in 2 to 3 days at much higher levels
  • Antibodies bind with greater affinity
  • Antibody level can remain high for weeks to months

IgM, IgG and Pregnancy

  • IgM antibodies are the first to be produced against a new antigen
  • IgG antibodies are produced later and provide long-term immunity
  • IgMs cannot cross the placenta — if a fetus has foreign antigens that the mother does not possess, IgMs cannot attack it
  • However, in subsequent pregnancies, IgGs to that antigen could be produced right away, and those can easily cross the placenta
  • This is why we run titers on antibodies toward some of these more common antigens
Two-line summary: Primary response = slow (lag 3-6 days, peak 10 days), then declines. Secondary response = fast (memory cells respond in hours, peak 2-3 days), much higher titer, greater affinity, lasts weeks-to-months. The whole point of vaccines.

Vaccination HIGH YIELD

Purpose, Result, Bonus

  • Purpose: trigger the primary response without the symptoms of an infection in order to develop immunological memory
  • Result: when we encounter the real pathogen, our body can immediately mount a secondary response and efficiently clear the infection (usually before symptoms are even felt)
  • Bonus: with an appropriate formulation and immunization schedule, vaccines produce a superior response compared to natural infection

Active vs Passive Immunity

NaturalArtificial
Active (your immune system makes its own response)InfectionVaccination
Passive (you receive preformed immunity made by another person)Maternal antibodyImmune globulin therapy

Natural = acquired through normal life experiences, not medical means. Artificial = produced purposefully through medical procedures (immunization).

Types of Vaccines

Whole-Organism Vaccines

  • Live-Attenuated: modify the pathogen to make it avirulent — able to replicate but unable to cause disease
  • Inactivated (Dead): use heat or chemical agents to kill the pathogen

Component Vaccines

Contain known, purified antigens from the pathogen:

  • Polysaccharide
  • Toxoid
  • Recombinant Antigen

Live-Attenuated Vaccines — Advantages

  • Because they grow and replicate in the host similarly to the natural infection, immunological memory is gained against all antigens expressed by the pathogen
  • Prolonged immune-system exposure creates a long-lasting immune response (lifetime)
  • Single, highly effective immunizations

Live-Attenuated Vaccines — Disadvantages

  • Possible reversion (regaining virulence) — vaccine-derived poliovirus infections occurred in the US, which is why we switched back to inactivated virus vaccines
  • Presence of other pathogens as contaminants/unforeseen reactions
  • Need for constant refrigeration — cold chain
  • Cannot be given to immunocompromised individuals

Component (Subunit) Vaccines — Advantages

  • Give a strong immune response against the antigen provided
  • Safe to use on immunocompromised individuals because no live pathogen is present

Component Vaccines — Disadvantages

  • Only builds immunological memory against the specific antigen given — requires careful selection of conserved antigens that don't readily mutate
  • Because they don't mimic the natural infection, booster shots are required for ongoing protection
Two-bucket framework: Whole-Organism (live-attenuated, inactivated) vs Component (polysaccharide, toxoid, recombinant). Live-attenuated = strong & lifelong but risky for immunocompromised; component = safe but needs boosters.

Epidemiology Basics

Definition

Epidemiology = the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

What Epidemiology Studies

  • Frequency — the number of health events and the relationship of that number to the size of the population
  • Pattern — the occurrence of health-related events by time, place, and person

John Snow — "Father of Epidemiology"

  • Wasn't first — Hippocrates (400 BC) tried to link environment and host factors to disease
  • Snow established the modern techniques used in epidemiology
  • 1854: cholera outbreak in Golden Square, London

Snow's Approach (Two Types of Epidemiology)

  1. Descriptive epidemiology: made a spot map — marked where cholera victims lived, overlaid local water pumps, noticed correlation
  2. Convinced city officials to remove the handle from the implicated pump — epidemic ended
  3. Analytic epidemiology (hypothesis testing): retrospectively examined which water company supplied each victim — found downstream water (Southwark & Vauxhall) had 5.0 deaths/1,000 vs upstream water (Lambeth) with 0.9 deaths/1,000

Two Types of Epidemiology

TypeWhat It DoesSnow Example
DescriptiveDescribes what's happening, where, when, to whomSpot map of cholera deaths
AnalyticTests hypotheses about causeComparing death rates between upstream/downstream water companies

What Epidemiology Tells You About a Pathogen

  1. Who? — collects information about patients; can identify if a disease targets an age, gender, or habit
  2. How infectious? — allows identification of the basic reproductive number (R0)

Herd Immunity, R0, & Vaccine Efficacy

Basic Reproductive Number (R0)

R0 = the average number of secondary cases generated by one primary case in a fully susceptible population.

R0 valueWhat it means
R0 = 1A primary case generates exactly 1 new case (steady state)
R0 > 1Expansion — pool of infected individuals grows (epidemic)
R0 < 1Shrinking pool — outbreak dies out

Herd Immunity

  • State where enough of the population is vaccinated/immunized against a pathogen that transmission stops
  • The presence of immunized people breaks the chain of transmission
  • The more infectious the disease, the higher the percentage needed
  • Protects people who cannot be vaccinated: too young, too old, or immunocompromised

The Herd Immunity Formula

Herd immunity threshold = (1 − (1/R₀)) × 100%

Worked Examples

Vaccine Efficacy (VE)

Epidemiology principles can also assess how well a vaccine works.

VE (%) = (1 − RV / RU) × 100 VE = Vaccine Efficacy RV = incidence / attack rate in vaccinated people RU = incidence / attack rate in unvaccinated people As RV shrinks (vaccinated people get sick less often), VE goes UP

Worked Example

If 100 vaccinated people had a 5% attack rate (RV = 0.05) and 100 unvaccinated had a 50% attack rate (RU = 0.50):

VE = (1 − 0.05/0.50) × 100 = (1 − 0.10) × 100 = 90% effective

Why didn't COVID herd immunity happen? Because the R0 kept rising as new variants emerged (3 → 8 → 18.6), the threshold rose from 66% to nearly 95%. We never reached vaccination coverage that high in time, especially globally.
