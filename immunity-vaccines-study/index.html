Two Branches of Immunity

Despite living in a world teeming with microbes, we are rarely infected — thanks to our immune system. It has two main branches that work together.

FeatureInnate ImmunityAdaptive Immunity
AvailabilityAlways available (constitutive)Set in motion by contact with a particular agent
SpeedFast (minutes–hours)Slow at first (days–weeks)
SpecificityBroad (recognizes MAMPs)Highly specific (recognizes individual antigens)
MemoryNoneYES — stronger response on re-exposure
ComponentsSkin, mucous membranes, AMPs, lysozyme, complement, phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells)B cells (humoral / antibodies), T cells (cell-mediated)

The Cascade: Innate → Adaptive

  • Innate starts with the skin, then resident immune cells (macrophages, dendritic cells)
  • When trouble is sensed, they call for backup → circulating cells (neutrophils) join the fight
  • If infection is bad, dendritic cells activate the adaptive response
  • T-cell activation → more T-cells + activation of B-cells
  • B and T cells then enhance the innate response to clear the infection

Three Factors That Decide Disease Outcome

  1. Inoculum size (number of invading microbes)
  2. Virulence (invasive ability of the agent)
  3. Strength of host defenses

If virulence is high (e.g. Shigella), required inoculum is low. If host defenses are strong, even a high inoculum may be cleared.

Think of it like a relay race: skin/mucous → complement → phagocytes → dendritic cells present antigen → T cells activate → B cells make antibodies. Each step buys time and amplifies the next.

Innate Immunity: Barriers to Entry

1. Mechanical Barrier — Skin

Like a massive wall physically keeping microbes out.

2. Antimicrobial Substances

  • Skin: high salt, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), and fatty acids (made by normal skin bacteria)
  • Mucous membranes (respiratory, digestive, genitourinary): antibodies, lysozyme, AMPs
  • Stomach acid (low pH): kills most ingested microbes
  • Bile salts in intestine
  • Complement in circulation (major factor)

3. Physical Currents That Sweep Microbes Away

  • Respiratory cilia — ciliated epithelial cells beat mucus + trapped microbes upward (mucociliary escalator)
  • Peristalsis — intestinal contractions move microbes through the GI tract
  • Urinary flow — flushes microbes out of the urinary tract

What Triggers the Innate Response?

Pattern-recognition receptors detect MAMPs (microbe-associated molecular patterns) — conserved microbial features that are essential for fitness, so microbes can't easily evolve away from them.

Common MAMPs

  • Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) — gram-negative outer membrane
  • Peptidoglycan — bacterial cell wall
  • Flagellin — flagellum protein
  • Pilin — pilus protein

The human innate immune system can recognize ~1,000 distinct MAMPs. How we avoid constant inflammation despite our microbiota is still not fully understood.

Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs)

ReceptorLocationWhat It Does
TLR (Toll-like receptor)Cell surface, facing outwardBinds extracellular MAMPs → activates NF-κB → cytokine genes + cell survival
NLR (NOD-like receptor)CytoplasmDetects MAMPs that breached the phagosome → can do 3 things: NF-κB activation, inflammasome assembly → pyroptosis, neutrophil chemotaxis
Mnemonic: TLRs face OUT (look at extracellular world). NLRs face IN (look at cytoplasm). Both signal through NF-κB to "sound the alarm."

Inflammation

The host's most powerful reaction to an invading agent. Characterized by reddening, swelling, pain, and pus.

The Four Cardinal Signs

SignCause
ReddeningIncreased blood flow to the area
SwellingTissues engorged with fluid containing immune effectors
PainRelease of chemical mediators + compression of nerves
PusDead white blood cells

Recruitment of WBCs

  1. Complement activation generates chemotaxins at the infection site
  2. WBCs in the bloodstream squeeze through the blood vessel walls — this is diapedesis
  3. WBCs move up the chemotactic gradient to the infection
  4. WBCs encounter bacteria and phagocytose them

Disease = Damage to Host Tissue

Damage can come from:

  • The microbe directly (e.g. toxins)
  • The host cell triggering its own death
  • The host's own immune response via inflammation — often the biggest source of damage!

Examples of Host Damage

Phagocytes — The Innate Killers

Phagocytes are cells with the innate ability to engulf foreign cells. Phagocytosis is one of the most effective innate defenses — the majority of bacteria invading humans are killed this way.

Steps in Phagocytosis

  1. Pseudopods engulf the microbe → trapped in a vesicle called the phagosome
  2. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome → forms the phagolysosome
  3. Microbe is digested by hydrolytic enzymes + oxidative enzymes + AMPs in the phagolysosome

Neutrophils — First Responders

  • Most numerous phagocytes
  • Short-lived (~1 week), cannot proliferate (terminally differentiated)
  • On a "seek and destroy" mission
  • Loaded with lysosomal granules + oxidative enzymes that make hypochlorite (bleach!)
  • Recruited by chemotaxins (complement-derived + bacterial products)
  • Require opsonins to be effective
  • Can also form NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps) — choose to die and release DNA + antimicrobials to entrap pathogens

Macrophages & Monocytes — The Coordinators

  • Monocytes = long-lived; circulate in blood; can differentiate further
  • Macrophages = monocytes that have entered tissues
  • Arrive at the site of infection more slowly than neutrophils
  • Act as "garbage collectors" — clean up microbial debris and remaining microbes after the neutrophil-microbe battle
  • Activated by cytokines
  • Produce cytokines that attract more neutrophils
  • Help initiate cell-mediated (adaptive) immunity by presenting antigen to T cells

Dendritic Cells — The Bridge

  • Specialized for antigen presentation
  • Sample antigens at infection sites, then migrate to lymph nodes
  • Activate T cells — this is the spark that lights the adaptive immune response

The Complement System

A soluble (not cell-based) defense system in the blood made of ~30 different proteins. Triggered by MAMPs and antibodies.

Three Activation Pathways

PathwayTrigger
ClassicalAntigen-antibody complex (links to adaptive immunity!)
LectinPathogens bound by host lectin (mannose-binding protein)
AlternativeSurface of pathogen directly (no antibody needed)

All three pathways converge on C3 convertase, which sets off a sequential proteolytic cascade. The cleavage products are pharmacologically active peptides that coordinate the inflammatory response.

The 3 Outputs of Complement

  1. Inflammation & phagocyte recruitment — C3a, C4a, C5a are mediators of inflammation and chemotaxins
  2. Opsonization — C3b coats the pathogen to make it palatable for phagocytes
  3. Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) — C5b + C6, 7, 8, 9 assemble pores in foreign membranes → lyses prokaryotic cells, eukaryotic cells, and even enveloped viruses

Opsonization

  • At rest, phagocytes are "sluggish eaters"
  • Opsonins (C3b) coat invading bacteria, making them more palatable
  • Antibodies (from the adaptive response) can also act as opsonins
  • Opsonin receptors on the phagocyte interact with the coating in a zipper-like fashion until the prey is engulfed

Avoiding Self-Attack

Host cells display sialic acid on their surfaces — this is the "self" tag that prevents complement from attacking host membranes. Without it, MAC would attack host cells (auto-immunity).

Mnemonic: Complement does 3 things — "I.O.M." — Inflammation, Opsonization, MAC. C3a/C5a recruit phagocytes; C3b coats them for eating; C5b-C9 punches a hole.

T-Cell Functions HIGH YIELD

T cells (T lymphocytes) are responsible for cell-mediated immunity. They mature in the thymus (that's the "T"). They don't make antibodies — they kill infected cells and direct the rest of the immune response.

The T-Cell Receptor (TCR)

  • Each T cell has a unique T-cell receptor (TCR) on its surface
  • The TCR cannot see free antigen — it only recognizes antigen that has been processed and presented on MHC molecules
  • This is fundamentally different from B cells, which can bind free antigen

MHC: The Display Case

MHC ClassFound OnPresentsTo Which T Cell
MHC IAll nucleated host cellsPeptides from cytoplasm (e.g. viruses, intracellular bacteria)CD8+ Cytotoxic T cells
MHC IIAntigen-presenting cells (APCs): dendritic cells, macrophages, B cellsPeptides from engulfed/extracellular pathogensCD4+ Helper T cells

Memory trick: MHC I × CD8 = 8 (1×8). MHC II × CD4 = 8 (2×4). Both equal 8.

T-Cell Activation (3 Signals)

For a naive T cell to become activated by a dendritic cell:

  1. Signal 1: TCR binds antigen-MHC complex on the APC
  2. Signal 2: Co-stimulation (CD28 on T cell binds B7 on APC) — confirms "real infection"
  3. Signal 3: Cytokines from APC tell the T cell what kind of effector to become

Without all 3 signals, the T cell becomes anergic (unresponsive) — this prevents autoimmunity.

The Two Main T-Cell Types

1. CD4+ Helper T Cells (TH) — The Conductors

  • Recognize antigen on MHC II
  • Don't kill directly; they coordinate the rest of the immune response via cytokines
  • Activate B cells → antibody production (this is critical for the antibody response!)
  • Activate macrophages through IFN-γ → activated macrophages have:
    • Increased metabolism & lysosomal proteins
    • Production of cytokines & growth factors
    • Increased phagocytosis
  • Subsets include TH1 (helps cell-mediated immunity), TH2 (helps antibody response), TH17 (anti-fungal/extracellular bacteria), Treg (suppress immune response, prevent autoimmunity)

2. CD8+ Cytotoxic T Cells (CTL or TC) — The Assassins

  • Recognize antigen on MHC I
  • Kill infected host cells — especially virus-infected cells and tumor cells
  • Killing mechanisms:
    • Perforin — punches holes in target membrane
    • Granzymes — enter through pores and trigger apoptosis
    • Fas ligand — binds Fas on target → apoptosis
  • Lecture description: "Kill antigen-presenting cells" (i.e. infected cells displaying foreign antigen on MHC I)

Memory T Cells

After the infection is cleared, most effector T cells die, but a small population becomes memory T cells that persist for years. On re-exposure, they respond faster and stronger — the basis of immunological memory and vaccines.

Mnemonic: Helper = "Helps everyone, doesn't kill" (CD4, MHC II). Cytotoxic = "kills infected cells" (CD8, MHC I). Both are essential — helpers tell B cells what to do, cytotoxics handle the cells that are already infected.

B-Cell Functions HIGH YIELD

B cells (B lymphocytes) are responsible for humoral immunity — they make antibodies. They mature in the bone marrow (that's the "B").

The B-Cell Receptor (BCR)

  • Each B cell has a unique B-cell receptor on its surface — this is essentially a membrane-bound antibody
  • Unlike TCRs, the BCR can recognize free antigen directly — no MHC presentation needed
  • B cells can recognize antigens of any kind: proteins, sugars, lipids, nucleic acids

B-Cell Activation

  1. BCR binds free antigen (e.g. a piece of a bacterium)
  2. B cell internalizes the antigen, processes it, and presents pieces on its MHC II
  3. A CD4+ helper T cell that recognizes the same antigen binds the B cell's MHC II-antigen complex
  4. The helper T cell delivers cytokines (e.g. IL-4) → the B cell is fully activated
  5. Activated B cell proliferates (clonal expansion) and differentiates into:
    • Plasma cells — antibody-secreting factories
    • Memory B cells — long-lived; basis for faster response on re-exposure
Critical link: B cells need T cell help! This is why HIV (which kills CD4+ T cells) cripples antibody production too. No T-helpers = no antibody response to most antigens.

Plasma Cells

  • Differentiated B cells specialized for antibody secretion
  • Each plasma cell pumps out ~2,000 antibody molecules per second
  • All antibodies from one plasma cell are identical (same specificity)
  • Most live for days to weeks; some long-lived plasma cells in bone marrow can secrete antibodies for years

Memory B Cells

  • Long-lived (years to decades)
  • Carry the same BCR as the original activated B cell
  • On re-exposure to the same antigen, they activate faster and stronger than naive B cells
  • Produce mainly IgG (rather than IgM) and antibodies with much higher affinity
  • This is the cellular basis for the secondary antibody response and for vaccines

Clonal Selection

The body makes millions of B cells, each with a unique BCR (generated by random V(D)J recombination). When a pathogen arrives, only the few B cells whose BCRs happen to match it get selected and expanded — this is clonal selection. The same principle applies to T cells.

Mnemonic: B cells = antiBodies. They Bind antigen directly. They mature in Bone marrow. They become plasma cells (factories) and memory cells (insurance).

The Antibody Response HIGH YIELD

What Is an Antibody?

An antibody (also called an immunoglobulin, Ig) is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells that binds a specific antigen.

Antibody Structure

  • Y-shaped protein with 4 chains: two heavy + two light, held together by disulfide bonds
  • Variable region (Fab tips) — the two top "arms" with the antigen-binding sites; sequence varies between different antibodies
  • Constant region (Fc tail) — the bottom; defines antibody class and binds host cell receptors
  • Each antibody has 2 identical antigen-binding sites — can crosslink and clump pathogens
Fab Fab ←← Variable region \\ // antigen binding sites \\ // Y | ←← Constant region (Fc) | binds complement, phagocytes |

The 5 Antibody Classes (Isotypes)

ClassWhere FoundFunction
IgMBlood (pentamer); first antibody madePrimary response — first to appear; great at agglutination & complement activation
IgGBlood & tissues; most abundant in bloodSecondary response; opsonization, neutralization, complement; only Ig that crosses placenta → protects newborns
IgAMucous membranes (gut, respiratory, genitourinary); breast milk; saliva, tearsMucosal immunity; passed in breast milk to protect newborns
IgEBound to mast cells & basophilsAllergic reactions, anti-parasite (worm) response
IgDB cell surfaceBCR on naive B cells; function less well understood

Memory: "Makes me Go Around Everywhere Daily" or just memorize: M G A E D.

How Antibodies Fight Infection (4 Functions)

  1. Neutralization — antibodies bind toxins or virus surface proteins, blocking them from interacting with host cells (e.g. anti-tetanus antibodies block tetanus toxin)
  2. Opsonization — antibodies coat the pathogen; phagocytes have Fc receptors that recognize the constant region → "eat me" signal (more efficient phagocytosis)
  3. Complement activation — antibody-antigen complexes trigger the classical complement pathway → MAC, C3b opsonization, C3a/C5a inflammation
  4. Agglutination & precipitation — because antibodies have 2 binding sites, they crosslink antigens into clumps that are easier to phagocytose

Primary vs Secondary Antibody Response

FeaturePrimary ResponseSecondary Response
When?First exposure to antigenRe-exposure (same antigen)
Lag timeSlow (~5–10 days)Fast (~1–3 days)
Antibody typeMostly IgM first, then some IgGMostly IgG (also IgA/IgE)
Antibody amountLower titerMuch higher titer (often 100×)
AffinityLowerHigher (affinity maturation)
DurationShorterLonger-lasting
Why?Naive B cells must be selected and expanded from scratchMemory B cells ready to go from prior exposure
The secondary response is the WHOLE point of vaccines. By exposing you to a harmless version of an antigen first, the vaccine creates memory B cells (and T cells). When the real pathogen arrives, your immune system mounts the fast/strong secondary response and clears the infection before it takes hold.

Antibody Diversity: V(D)J Recombination

  • The variable regions of antibodies are generated by random recombination of V, D, and J gene segments during B-cell development
  • This produces an estimated 1011 unique antibody specificities — far more than any pathogen we'll ever meet
  • Affinity maturation — during the secondary response, B cells in lymph nodes undergo more mutations, and the highest-affinity ones are selected
  • Class switching — activated B cells can switch from making IgM to IgG/IgA/IgE while keeping the same specificity

Vaccines HIGH YIELD

How Vaccines Work

A vaccine exposes the immune system to a harmless version of an antigen — either the whole pathogen weakened/killed, or pieces of it. This triggers a primary immune response and generates memory B and T cells without causing disease.

If you later encounter the real pathogen, your memory cells mount a fast, strong secondary response that clears the infection before symptoms develop.

No vaccine: Real pathogen arrives → slow primary response (5-10 days) → disease With vaccine: Vaccine → primary response → memory cells formed | v Real pathogen arrives → fast secondary response (1-3 days) → no disease

Adjuvants

Many vaccines include an adjuvant (e.g. aluminum salts) — an ingredient that enhances the immune response. Adjuvants typically work by triggering innate pattern-recognition receptors (mimicking a real infection's "danger signal"), which boosts T- and B-cell activation.

Active = your immune system does the work and remembers. Passive = you receive ready-made antibodies; immediate but no memory.

Herd Immunity

  • When a large enough fraction of the population is immune, the pathogen can't sustain transmission
  • Protects people who cannot be vaccinated — immunocompromised patients, infants too young for vaccines, people with allergies to vaccine components
  • The threshold depends on the pathogen's R0 (basic reproductive number):
    • Measles (R0 ≈ 12–18) needs ~95% coverage
    • Polio (R0 ≈ 5–7) needs ~80–85%
    • Influenza (R0 ≈ 1–2) needs ~50–75%

Vaccine Successes

  • Smallpox — only human disease ever eradicated, declared eliminated in 1980
  • Polio — near-eradication; only a few cases per year worldwide
  • Measles, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, tetanus — rates dropped dramatically in vaccinated populations

Why Some Vaccines Are Hard

  • Antigenic variation — pathogens like influenza and HIV change their surface proteins constantly → antibodies from a previous strain don't recognize the new one
  • Latency — HIV, herpesviruses hide inside host cells where antibodies can't reach
  • Immune evasion — some pathogens (TB, malaria) actively suppress immune responses
  • Many serotypes — e.g. there are >100 strains of rhinovirus (common cold)
Big picture: Vaccines exploit the secondary response. They trick your immune system into making memory B and T cells against a harmless preview of the pathogen. The first real exposure feels like a second exposure, so it's cleared before causing disease.
