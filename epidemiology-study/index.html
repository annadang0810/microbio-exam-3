What is Epidemiology?

DEF Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

Two Things Epidemiology Studies

  • Frequency — the number of health events and the relationship of that number to the size of the population (i.e., a rate, not just a count)
  • Pattern — the occurrence of health-related events by time, place, and person
"Time, place, person" is the classic descriptive triad — remember it. Snow's 1854 spot map answered "place" + "person"; analytic epidemiology answers why.

What Epidemiology Tells Us About a Pathogen

  • Who? — collecting patient information reveals if a disease targets a particular age, gender, or behavior
  • How infectious? — studying spread reveals the basic reproduction number (R0)
  • Vaccination strategy? — epidemiology principles are also used to assess vaccine efficacy and to calculate the herd immunity threshold

John Snow & the 1854 Cholera Outbreak HIGH YIELD

John Snow is called the "Father of Epidemiology" — not because he was the first (Hippocrates tried in 400 BC), but because he established the modern techniques of the field.

The Outbreak

  • Year: 1854
  • Location: Golden Square area, London (Broad Street)
  • Disease: cholera (we now know it's caused by Vibrio cholerae, a gram-negative bacterium)

Step 1 — The Spot Map (Descriptive Epi)

  • Snow started by making a spot map — he marked where on the city map each cholera victim lived
  • Because he believed water was the source, he overlaid the locations of local water pumps
  • He noticed a correlation: cases clustered around a particular pump
  • This type of map (showing the geographic distribution of cases) is a form of descriptive epidemiology
A spot map describes where and who — it doesn't prove causation. It generates a hypothesis. That's why it's descriptive, not analytical.

Step 2 — The Famous Pump Handle

  • Snow showed his spot map to city officials, who removed the handle from the suspect pump
  • The epidemic ended
  • He now hypothesized that water was the cause of cholera

Step 3 — The Two Water Companies (Analytic Epi)

To test his hypothesis, Snow went back retrospectively to look at who got cholera in more detail. He found:

  • Two water companies served the area, both pulling water from the Thames river
  • Lambeth — intake UPSTREAM of London (cleaner)
  • Southwark & Vauxhall — intake DOWNSTREAM of London (contaminated by city sewage)
  • Cholera deaths were much higher where the water came from downstream

The Snow Comparison Table (1854)

The ~5× difference in death rate between the two single-company districts (5.0 vs 0.9 per 1,000) was strong evidence that contaminated water was causing cholera. Because this step was hypothesis-testing, it is analytic epidemiology.

Why Snow Matters

  • He pioneered the map-based descriptive approach — still used today (think: COVID dashboards)
  • He distinguished descriptive (hypothesis-generating) from analytic (hypothesis-testing) work
  • He showed that epidemiology can identify a cause without knowing the pathogen — he didn't know about V. cholerae; he just had populations, exposure data, and counts
Memorize the two stages: STAGE 1 = spot map (descriptive, generates hypothesis "water"). STAGE 2 = two-companies comparison (analytic, tests hypothesis using exposure groups).

Descriptive vs Analytical Epidemiology HIGH YIELD

Epidemiology has two distinct modes of investigation. Snow's two stages are the textbook example of each.

Descriptive Epidemiology

  • Asks: who, what, where, when
  • Describes the distribution of disease in a population — by time, place, and person
  • Generates hypotheses about possible causes; does NOT test them
  • Examples: spot map (Snow's first step), case counts, age/sex distributions, time trends, incidence/prevalence reports

Analytical Epidemiology

  • Asks: why
  • Tests hypotheses by comparing groups (exposed vs unexposed, vaccinated vs unvaccinated, etc.)
  • Looks for associations between an exposure and an outcome
  • Example: Snow's comparison of cholera death rates between districts served by the Southwark & Vauxhall (downstream, contaminated) and Lambeth (upstream, cleaner) water companies. He had a hypothesis ("water is the cause") and tested it by comparing groups.

R0 — The Basic Reproduction Number HIGH YIELD

DEF R0 = the average number of secondary cases generated by one primary case in a fully susceptible population.

R0 is a property of the pathogen + the population/setting, not just the pathogen alone — the same virus can have a different R0 in a dense city versus a remote village. But the assumption that the population is fully susceptible (no one is yet immune or vaccinated) is fixed in the definition.

What the Numbers Mean

R0 valueMeaningWhat happens to the outbreak
R0 = 1Each primary case generates exactly one new caseSteady state — outbreak persists at constant size. (A primary case must generate at least one new case for the disease to be sustained.)
R0 > 1Each case generates more than one new caseExpansion of infected individuals — the outbreak grows
R0 < 1Each case generates fewer than one new caseShrinking pool of infected individuals — the outbreak dies out

R0 Examples from the Lecture

Pathogen / VariantR0
Ebola~2
Original COVID-19 (Wuhan strain)3
COVID-19 Delta8
COVID-19 Omicron9
COVID-19 Omicron BA.518.6

Why R0 Matters

  • Tells us how infectious a pathogen is — useful for planning public health response
  • Lets us calculate the herd immunity threshold (see next section)
  • Lets us compare outbreaks: COVID Delta (R0 = 8) is more infectious than original COVID (R0 = 3) is more infectious than Ebola (R0 ~ 2)
If R0 < 1, the disease can't sustain itself — this is the goal of public health interventions like vaccination and quarantine. They effectively push R below 1 even if the pathogen's intrinsic R0 is high.

Herd Immunity HIGH YIELD

DEF Herd immunity = a state where enough of the population is vaccinated/immunized against a pathogen that transmission through the population stops.

How It Works

  • The presence of immunized people in the population breaks the chain of transmission — an infected person is more likely to bump into someone who can't be infected, so the chain stops
  • The more infectious the disease (higher R0), the higher the percentage of the population that needs to be vaccinated to achieve herd immunity
  • Herd immunity protects people who cannot be vaccinated: too young, too old, or immunocompromised (e.g., chemo patients, organ transplant recipients)

The Herd Immunity Formula

Herd Immunity Threshold = (1 − 1/R0) × 100% R0 = basic reproduction number
The result is the minimum % of the population that must be immune to break transmission

Worked Examples (from the lecture)

PathogenR0CalculationHerd immunity %
Ebola2(1 − 1/2) × 100%50%
Original COVID-193(1 − 1/3) × 100%66%
COVID-19 Delta8(1 − 1/8) × 100%87.5%
COVID-19 Omicron BA.518.6(1 − 1/18.6) × 100%94.6%

Why It's Hard for Highly Infectious Pathogens

For BA.5, you'd need ~95% of the entire population immune to stop transmission. With waning immunity, vaccine hesitancy, and unvaccinated children, that bar is extremely difficult to clear — which is why COVID continued to circulate even with widespread vaccination.

The intuition: at the threshold, an infected person on average meets fewer than one susceptible contact — so the effective R drops below 1 and the outbreak dies. The math: when fraction p are immune, R = R0(1−p). Setting R = 1 and solving for p gives p = 1 − 1/R0.

Vaccine Efficacy (VE) HIGH YIELD

DEF Vaccine efficacy measures how well a vaccine reduces the incidence (or attack rate) of disease in a vaccinated group, compared with an unvaccinated group, under controlled trial conditions.

The Vaccine Efficacy Formula

VE (%) = (1 − RV / RU) × 100 RV = incidence (or attack rate) in vaccinated people
RU = incidence (or attack rate) in unvaccinated people
As RV gets smaller, VE increases.

An attack rate is the proportion of people who got sick out of the people at risk — e.g., if 5 of 1,000 vaccinated people got the disease, RV = 0.005 (or 0.5%).

What VE Tells You

  • VE = 100%: the vaccine is perfect — nobody vaccinated got sick (RV = 0)
  • VE = 90%: vaccinated people had 90% lower incidence than unvaccinated — very good
  • VE = 50%: vaccinated people had half the incidence of unvaccinated
  • VE = 0%: vaccine had no effect (RV = RU)
  • VE < 0%: rare, but would mean vaccinated people got more sick — suggests a problem

Worked Example

In a vaccine trial, 1,000 vaccinated people and 1,000 unvaccinated people are followed. After one year:

  • 10 vaccinated people get sick → RV = 10/1000 = 0.01 (1%)
  • 100 unvaccinated people get sick → RU = 100/1000 = 0.10 (10%)
VE = (1 − RV/RU) × 100 = (1 − 0.01/0.10) × 100 = (1 − 0.1) × 100 = 90%

Important Distinctions

  • Vaccine efficacy = measured under controlled trial conditions (RCT) — the answer to "does this work?"
  • Vaccine effectiveness = measured in the real world — the answer to "how well does it work in practice?"
  • The lecture uses the same formula for both; just note that the input data (RV, RU) come from a trial vs. real-world surveillance respectively
Quick gut check: if RV is half of RU, VE = 50%. If RV is one-tenth of RU, VE = 90%. If RV is one-hundredth of RU, VE = 99%. The smaller RV is relative to RU, the higher the VE.

Calculations Cheat Sheet HIGH YIELD

Your professor said you need to know how to calculate these. Here are the only two formulas you must memorize, with practice problems.

Formula 1 — Herd Immunity Threshold

HIT = (1 − 1/R0) × 100%

Practice: Herd Immunity

Q1. Measles has R0 = 15. What % of the population must be immune for herd immunity?

HIT = (1 − 1/15) × 100% = (1 − 0.0667) × 100% = 0.9333 × 100% = 93.3%

Q2. Influenza has R0 ≈ 1.3. Herd immunity threshold?

HIT = (1 − 1/1.3) × 100% = (1 − 0.769) × 100% ≈ 23%

Q3. A new pathogen has R0 = 4. HIT?

HIT = (1 − 1/4) × 100% = (1 − 0.25) × 100% = 75%

Formula 2 — Vaccine Efficacy

VE (%) = (1 − RV / RU) × 100

Practice: Vaccine Efficacy

Q4. In a trial, 50 of 5,000 vaccinated people get sick; 250 of 5,000 unvaccinated people get sick. VE?

RV = 50/5000 = 0.01 RU = 250/5000 = 0.05 VE = (1 − 0.01/0.05) × 100 = (1 − 0.2) × 100 = 80%

Q5. 5 of 1,000 vaccinated people get sick; 100 of 1,000 unvaccinated. VE?

RV = 5/1000 = 0.005 RU = 100/1000 = 0.10 VE = (1 − 0.005/0.10) × 100 = (1 − 0.05) × 100 = 95%

Q6. A vaccine has VE = 70%. If the unvaccinated attack rate is RU = 0.20, what is RV?

VE = (1 − RV/RU) × 100 70 = (1 − RV/0.20) × 100 0.70 = 1 − RV/0.20 RV/0.20 = 0.30 RV = 0.30 × 0.20 = 0.06 (6%)

Combo Problem

Q7. Pathogen X has R0 = 5. A vaccine for X has VE = 80% (RV = 0.02, RU = 0.10). (a) What is the herd immunity threshold? (b) Even if 100% of the population is vaccinated, can we achieve true sterilizing herd immunity with this vaccine?

(a) HIT = (1 − 1/5) × 100% = 80% (b) Even at 100% coverage, only 80% of vaccinated people are effectively protected (VE = 80%) → effective immune fraction = 80%. That just barely meets the threshold — very fragile. Any drop in coverage means transmission can resume.
Watch out for unit traps: incidence/attack rates are usually given as proportions (0.05) or percentages (5%). Make sure RV and RU are in the same units before dividing.

The "Steps" Algorithm (use this on the exam)

  • Step 1: Identify the formula. "How many need vaccinated?" → HIT. "How well does the vaccine work?" → VE.
  • Step 2: Pull out the numbers. For HIT: R0. For VE: count cases ÷ total in each group to get RV and RU.
  • Step 3: Plug into the formula exactly as written: (1 − ...) × 100.
  • Step 4: Sanity check — is your answer between 0 and 100%? Does a more infectious R0 give a higher HIT? (It should.) Does a more effective vaccine give a higher VE? (It should.)
