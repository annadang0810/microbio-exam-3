Definitions & Adaptive Immunity Functions

Key Definitions

TermMeaning
Immune systemThe cells, tissues, and molecules that mediate resistance to infections
ImmunityResistance of a host to pathogens and their toxic effects
Immune responseCollective and coordinated response to introduction of foreign substances, mediated by cells and molecules of the immune system
AntigenSelf or non-self substance that elicits an immune response — mostly large, complex molecules (e.g. O-antigen of LPS)

3 Major Functions of Adaptive Immunity

  1. Recognize non-self — distinguish self from non-self
  2. Respond to non-self — effector response eliminates or renders foreign material harmless
  3. Remember non-self — anamnestic response: on second encounter, mount a faster and more intense response

Two Branches of Adaptive Immunity

BranchCellsTargets
Cell-Mediated Immune Response (CMIR)T-cellsEliminates intracellular microbes that survive within phagocytes or other infected cells
Humoral Immune Response (HIR)B-cells (via antibodies)Eliminates extracellular microbes and their toxins

Adaptive Enhances Innate

Adaptive immunity isn't separate from innate — it enhances the innate response. Example: T-cells produce IFN-γ → activates macrophages → increased metabolism, lysosomal proteins, cytokine production, and phagocytosis.

Active vs Passive Immunity HIGH YIELD

Acquired (adaptive) immunity comes in two flavors — active (your immune system does the work) or passive (you receive pre-made antibodies). Each can be acquired naturally (life experience) or artificially (medical procedure).

The 4-Box Grid

NATURAL (life experience)ARTIFICIAL (medical / immunization)
ACTIVE
(you make antibodies)		 Natural infection — you catch the disease and your own immune system makes antibodies + memory cells Vaccination — you receive an antigen, your immune system mounts a primary response and makes memory
PASSIVE
(pre-made antibodies)		 Maternal antibodies — IgG crosses placenta to fetus; IgA passes in breast milk Immune globulin therapy — injection of pre-made antibodies (e.g. anti-venom, IVIG, monoclonal antibodies)

Active Immunity

  • Your immune system does the work — B and T cells are activated, expand, and form memory
  • Slow to start (primary response takes days), but produces long-lasting memory (years to lifetime)
  • On re-exposure, secondary response is fast and strong

Passive Immunity

  • You receive preformed antibodies made by someone else
  • Immediate protection — no waiting for primary response
  • No memory cells generated — antibodies degrade over weeks to months and you're back to square one
  • Useful when there's no time for active immunity (post-exposure to rabies, snake bite, immunocompromised patient)
ACTIVE — you make it → memory → long-lasting slow at first, but protected for years PASSIVE — you receive it → no memory → short-term instant protection, but gone in weeks-months
Memory hook: A=Active=Antibodies you make. P=Passive=Pre-made antibodies. Active builds memory; passive does not. Natural happens through life; artificial requires a medical intervention.

Examples in the 4 Boxes

  • Natural Active: catching chickenpox in childhood and being immune for life
  • Artificial Active: getting the MMR vaccine
  • Natural Passive: a newborn protected by mom's IgG (placenta) and IgA (breast milk) for the first ~6 months
  • Artificial Passive: rabies immune globulin after a bite, snake antivenom, IVIG for immunodeficient patients, monoclonal antibodies (e.g. Rituximab)

T-cell Activation: MHC I & MHC II HIGH YIELD

T cells can't see free antigen — they only recognize antigen that has been processed and presented on a Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) molecule. The MHC is essential for telling self from non-self.

The Two Classes of MHC

FeatureMHC Class IMHC Class II
Found onALL nucleated cellsOnly antigen-presenting cells (APCs) — macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells
Source of antigenEndogenous (originates inside the cell)Exogenous (originates outside the cell)
Type of pathogenViruses, intracellular bacteria, tumor proteinsEngulfed extracellular bacteria, debris, parasites
Presented toCD8+ Cytotoxic T cells (TC)CD4+ Helper T cells (TH)
OutcomeTC kills the infected cellTH coordinates the response (activates B cells, macrophages)

Memory trick: MHC I × CD8 = 8 (1×8). MHC II × CD4 = 8 (2×4). Both products = 8.

Endogenous Antigen Processing (MHC I)

For antigens that originate inside the cell's cytoplasm (e.g. viral proteins made by an infected cell):

  1. Antigen is in the cytoplasm
  2. Loaded onto MHC Class I
  3. Displayed on the cell surface
  4. Recognized by CD8+ Cytotoxic T cells
  5. TC kills the infected cell

Exogenous Antigen Processing (MHC II)

For antigens that originate outside the cell and are engulfed by an APC:

  1. APC engulfs the foreign microbe (phagosome)
  2. Microbe is broken down into peptides
  3. Peptides loaded onto MHC Class II
  4. Displayed on the APC surface
  5. Recognized by CD4+ Helper T cells
  6. TH activates B cells, macrophages, and other arms of the response

Why MHC II Is Limited to APCs

MHC II is required for T-cell communication with macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells. These are the cells that need to recruit T-helper cells to coordinate the immune response — a B cell, for example, needs T-helper cytokines to fully activate and make antibodies.

The Cell-Mediated Response (3 Steps)

  1. T-cell recognizes antigen bound to MHC
  2. When non-self antigen is detected, T-cell is activated to kill infected cells
  3. Activated T-cells undergo clonal expansion to make many copies of themselves
Big picture: MHC I = "I'm infected, kill me" (signal to CD8+ killers). MHC II = "Help me, I have invaders" (signal to CD4+ helpers). The cell type displaying the MHC tells the T cell what kind of antigen it's looking at — intracellular vs extracellular.

T-Cell Education: Positive & Negative Selection HIGH YIELD

T-cell education has two stages: positive selection (must work) and negative selection (must not be self-reactive). Failure at either stage = apoptosis.

Stage 1 — Positive Selection

  • Goal: enrich for T-cells that are functional — can recognize self-MHC molecules with good affinity
  • If the TCR recognizes self-MHC → SURVIVAL (proceeds to negative selection)
  • If the TCR fails to recognize self-MHC → APOPTOSIS

Stage 2 — Negative Selection

  • Goal: eliminate T-cells that would attack the body's own cells (self-reactive) → tolerance
  • If the TCR binds self-antigen on self-MHC TOO TIGHTLY → APOPTOSIS
  • If the TCR fails to bind self-antigen tightly → SURVIVAL and continued maturation

The Affinity Goldilocks Zone

TCR-Self MHC affinityOutcome
Low affinityApoptosis (failed positive selection)
Good / appropriate affinitySurvival — mature T cell released
Too high affinityApoptosis (failed negative selection)
Positive selection enriches for functional T-cells (recognize self-MHC). Negative selection eliminates self-reactive T-cells. The survivors recognize self-MHC but only respond when there's a foreign peptide on it.

Primary vs Secondary Humoral Response HIGH YIELD

Humoral Immune Response (HIR) Basics

  1. B-cells recognize specific antigens via their B-cell receptor (BCR)
  2. Activated B-cells proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells
  3. Antibodies bind to specific antigens on microbes
  4. Some B-cells become memory cells — primed to respond faster on re-exposure

Antibody Structure

  • Y-shaped immunoglobulins (Igs) secreted by plasma cells
  • Variable region — the tips; binds specifically to antigen detected by the B cell
  • Constant region — defines the Ig class
  • Five classes: IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, IgE

Primary Humoral Response

  • Cell proliferation and differentiation upon first antigen exposure
  • Lag period: 3 to 6 days — nothing detectable for the first few days
  • Peak plasma antibody levels reached at ~10 days
  • Antibody levels then decline
  • Mostly IgM first, then some IgG appears

Secondary Humoral Response

  • Re-exposure to the same antigen gives a faster, more prolonged, more effective response
  • Sensitized memory cells respond within HOURS
  • Antibody levels peak in 2–3 days at much higher levels
  • Antibodies bind with greater affinity (affinity maturation)
  • Antibody level can remain high for weeks to months
  • Mostly IgG (class-switched from IgM)

IgM vs IgG (Especially Pregnancy)

  • IgM antibodies are the first to be produced against a new antigen (primary response)
  • IgG is produced later and provides long-term immunity
  • IgM CANNOT cross the placenta (it's a pentamer, too big)
  • So if a fetus has foreign antigens that the mother does not possess, IgM cannot attack it (safe in first pregnancy)
  • However, in subsequent pregnancies, IgGs to that antigen can be produced quickly (secondary response) and can easily cross the placenta — this can attack the fetus
  • This is why doctors run titers on antibodies toward common antigens in pregnancy (e.g. Rh factor)
Vaccines exploit the secondary response. The vaccine acts as the "first exposure" so the real pathogen feels like a SECOND exposure — secondary response (fast, IgG, high titer) clears it before symptoms appear.

Vaccine Types: Whole-Organism vs Component HIGH YIELD

Purpose of Vaccination

  • Purpose: trigger the primary response without the symptoms of an infection in order to develop immunological memory
  • Result: when we encounter the real pathogen, our body can immediately mount a secondary response and efficiently clear the infection (usually before symptoms are felt)
  • Bonus: with the right formulation and schedule, vaccines can produce a SUPERIOR response compared to natural infection

Two Big Categories

Live-Attenuated Vaccines — Advantages

  • Because they grow and replicate in the host similarly to the natural infection, immunological memory is gained against ALL antigens expressed by the pathogen
  • Prolonged immune-system exposure creates a long-lasting immune response (lifetime)
  • Single, highly effective immunizations — often only one or two doses needed

Live-Attenuated Vaccines — Disadvantages

  • Possible reversion (regaining virulence) — recently, vaccine-derived poliovirus infections occurred in the US, which is why we switched back to inactivated polio vaccines
  • Presence of other pathogens as contaminants / unforeseen reactions
  • Need for constant refrigeration — "Cold Chain" requirement
  • CANNOT be given to immunocompromised individuals — even attenuated, the pathogen can cause disease in someone with no immune defenses

Component (Subunit) Vaccines — Advantages

  • Strong immune response against the antigen provided
  • SAFE for immunocompromised individuals — no live pathogen present, can't cause disease

Component (Subunit) Vaccines — Disadvantages

  • Only builds immunological memory against the SPECIFIC ANTIGEN given — if the pathogen mutates that antigen, your immunity fails
  • Requires careful selection of conserved antigens that don't readily mutate
  • Because they don't mimic natural infection, booster shots are required for ongoing protection

Epidemiology Basics

Definition

Epidemiology = the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

What Epidemiology Studies

  • Frequency — the number of health events and the relationship of that number to the size of the population
  • Pattern — the occurrence of health-related events by time, place, and person

John Snow — "Father of Epidemiology"

  • Wasn't first — Hippocrates (400 BC) tried to link environment and host factors to disease
  • Snow established the modern techniques used in epidemiology
  • 1854: cholera outbreak in Golden Square, London

Snow's Approach (Two Types of Epidemiology)

  1. Descriptive epidemiology: made a spot map — marked where cholera victims lived, overlaid local water pumps, noticed correlation
  2. Convinced city officials to remove the handle from the implicated pump — epidemic ended
  3. Analytic epidemiology (hypothesis testing): retrospectively examined which water company supplied each victim — found downstream water (Southwark & Vauxhall) had 5.0 deaths/1,000 vs upstream water (Lambeth) with 0.9 deaths/1,000

Two Types of Epidemiology

TypeWhat It DoesSnow Example
DescriptiveDescribes what's happening, where, when, to whomSpot map of cholera deaths
AnalyticTests hypotheses about causeComparing death rates between upstream/downstream water companies

What Epidemiology Tells You About a Pathogen

  1. Who? — collects information about patients; can identify if a disease targets an age, gender, or habit
  2. How infectious? — allows identification of the basic reproductive number (R0)

Herd Immunity, R0, & Vaccine Efficacy

Basic Reproductive Number (R0)

R0 = the average number of secondary cases generated by one primary case in a fully susceptible population.

R0 valueWhat it means
R0 = 1A primary case generates exactly 1 new case (steady state)
R0 > 1Expansion — pool of infected individuals grows (epidemic)
R0 < 1Shrinking pool — outbreak dies out

Herd Immunity

  • State where enough of the population is vaccinated/immunized against a pathogen that transmission stops
  • The presence of immunized people breaks the chain of transmission
  • The more infectious the disease, the higher the percentage needed
  • Protects people who cannot be vaccinated: too young, too old, or immunocompromised

The Herd Immunity Formula

Herd immunity threshold = (1 − (1/R₀)) × 100%

Vaccine Efficacy (VE)

Epidemiology principles can also assess how well a vaccine works.

VE (%) = (1 − RV / RU) × 100 VE = Vaccine Efficacy RV = incidence / attack rate in vaccinated people RU = incidence / attack rate in unvaccinated people As RV shrinks (vaccinated people get sick less often), VE goes UP

Worked Example

If 100 vaccinated people had a 5% attack rate (RV = 0.05) and 100 unvaccinated had a 50% attack rate (RU = 0.50):

VE = (1 − 0.05/0.50) × 100 = (1 − 0.10) × 100 = 90% effective

Why didn't COVID herd immunity happen? Because the R0 kept rising as new variants emerged (3 → 8 → 18.6), the threshold rose from 66% to nearly 95%. We never reached vaccination coverage that high in time, especially globally.
