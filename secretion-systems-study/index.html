The Compartment Problem

All proteins are made in the cytoplasm, but not all proteins function there. They need to get to one of several compartments:

  • Inner membrane (cytoplasmic / plasma membrane)
  • Periplasm (only in gram-negative bacteria)
  • Outer membrane (only in gram-negative bacteria)
  • Extracellular space (or directly into a host cell)

DEF Protein secretion = the translocation of proteins from the cytoplasm to any other compartment.

Gram-negative bacteria face the toughest topology problem — they have TWO membranes (inner + outer) plus a periplasm in between. This is why secretion systems are mostly studied in gram-negatives.

Signal Peptides (N-terminal Signal Sequences) HIGH YIELD

Proteins that are secreted have N-terminal signal sequences — short amino acid tags that act like a "shipping address" telling the cell where to send the protein.

Structure of a Signal Peptide

The signal sequence has two distinct regions:

  • Hydrophilic basic region (N-terminal end) — contains positively charged residues (Lys, Arg, His)
  • Hydrophobic region (toward the cleavage site) — contains nonpolar residues (Leu, Ala, Val, Ile, Pro)
N-terminal signal sequence (cleaved during/after secretion): NH₂ — [Lys-Lys-Arg] — [Leu-Ala-Val-Pro-Phe-Ala] — | — mature protein basic region hydrophobic region cleavage (hydrophilic) site

Why N-terminal?

  • Proteins are translated N-to-C by the ribosome
  • An N-terminal signal sequence appears first, while the rest of the protein is still being made
  • This lets the cell recognize and target the protein co-translationally — before it folds in the cytoplasm

Cleavage

  • The signal peptide is cleaved during/after secretion by signal peptidase (Lep)
  • Cleavage releases the mature protein in its destination compartment
  • Cleavage site is at the boundary between the hydrophobic region and the mature sequence (the "red residue" in the slide table)

Key concept: The signal sequence is the "address label." The Sec system (and other transporters) is the "postal service" that reads the label and delivers the protein. No signal sequence = protein stays in the cytoplasm (with one big exception: Type I/ABC).

The Sec System HIGH YIELD

The Sec system is the main route for proteins to journey beyond the cytoplasm into the inner membrane, periplasm, or beyond. Most secreted proteins use it.

Two Pathways into the Sec Channel

Pathway A — SRP (for membrane-anchored proteins)

  • The signal recognition particle (SRP) — made of the protein Ffh + the small RNA molecule ffs — binds the signal sequence as it emerges from the ribosome
  • SRP escorts the ribosome-protein complex to the SecYEG channel in the inner membrane
  • The protein gets inserted into the inner membrane
  • Some hydrophobic proteins skip SRP and self-insert (no help needed)

Pathway B — SecA / SecB (for proteins crossing the membrane)

  • SecA recognizes the N-terminal signal sequence as it comes off the ribosome and brings the protein to the SecYEG channel
  • The chaperone SecB binds the protein in the cytoplasm to prevent it from folding — only unfolded proteins fit through SecYEG
  • ATP hydrolysis by SecA powers translocation: the protein is threaded through the SecYEG channel
  • Once across, signal peptidase (Lep) cleaves off the signal peptide
  • The mature, folded protein is released into the periplasm (or beyond)

The Sec Players

ComponentRole
SecACytoplasmic ATPase; recognizes signal sequence + powers translocation
SecBCytoplasmic chaperone; keeps protein unfolded until translocation

SRP (Ffh + ffs)Targets ribosome with hydrophobic signal sequence to SecYEG

Why N-terminal Sec Targeting?

The N-terminal signal emerges from the ribosome first. SecA / SRP can grab it co-translationally and bring the rest of the still-being-made protein to the membrane before it folds in the cytoplasm. Once a protein folds, it can't fit through SecYEG.

Crossing to the Outer Membrane (Bam complex)

Gram-negatives need to get some proteins all the way to the outer membrane. After Sec dumps an OM protein into the periplasm:

  • The chaperone Skp binds the protein in the periplasm and protects it from the DegP protease (which would otherwise degrade it)
  • Skp escorts the protein to the Bam complex in the outer membrane
  • The chaperone SurA assists Bam in folding the protein and inserting it as a β-barrel into the outer membrane
Sec system summary: Signal sequence → SecA/SecB or SRP → SecYEG channel → ATP-driven threading → signal peptidase cleavage → mature protein in destination compartment. For OM proteins, add: Skp → Bam + SurA.

Type I Secretion System (T1SS) — ABC Exporter

Type I is the simplest extracellular secretion system. It's the one that does NOT use Sec.

Key Features

  • Sec INDEPENDENT — bypasses the Sec system entirely
  • Made of 3 proteins, one of which is the ABC transporter (ATPase binding cassette)
  • Operates on proteins that LACK a signal sequence
  • Protein is exported directly from the cytoplasm into the extracellular space — one step, no periplasmic intermediate
  • Uses ATP hydrolysis (via the ABC ATPase) to power transport
Type I (ABC exporter) — Sec independent Cytoplasm → ABC transporter → extracellular space (no signal seq required) (ATP-powered)
Easy way to remember: Type I is the "1-step" system — one ATPase pump, no Sec, no signal peptide, straight from cytoplasm to outside.

Type II Secretion System (T2SS) — Two-Step HIGH YIELD

Type II is the classic two-step extracellular secretion system. It piggybacks on the Sec system.

Key Features

  • Sec DEPENDENT — can't function without Sec
  • Two-step mechanism (the "two-step system")
  • Substrate: secreted proteins with N-terminal signal sequences

Step 1 — Cytoplasm to Periplasm

Protein moves from the cytoplasm to the periplasm using the Sec system (SecA/SecB → SecYEG). Signal peptide is cleaved.

Step 2 — Periplasm to Extracellular Space

14 accessory proteins form a complex that moves the now-folded protein across the outer membrane into the extracellular space.

Components in the Lecture Diagram

  • GspD — the outer membrane channel ("secretin")
  • GspC, GspM, GspL — inner membrane platform
  • GspE — cytoplasmic ATPase that powers the system
  • GspG, H, I, J, K — pseudopilus subunits that push the substrate through GspD
  • GspF — inner membrane platform protein
Type II (Two-step) — Sec dependent Step 1: Cytoplasm —[Sec]→ Periplasm Step 2: Periplasm —[14 accessory proteins / GspD pore]→ Extracellular
Mnemonic: Type II = "two-step." Sec gets it across the inner membrane, then the GspD machinery pushes it across the outer membrane.

Type III Secretion System (T3SS) — The Injectisome HIGH YIELD

The T3SS is a syringe-like nanomachine that injects bacterial proteins directly into a eukaryotic host cell. Major virulence factor in pathogens.

Key Features

  • Sec INDEPENDENT
  • Found in pathogenic gram-negative bacteria: Salmonella, Shigella, Yersinia, Pseudomonas
  • Spans both membranes (double-membrane-embedded)
  • Delivers effector proteins directly into the cytoplasm or plasma membrane of a target eukaryotic cell
  • Effectors subvert host cell functions — promote bacterial invasion and colonization

The Injectisome Structure (~25 proteins)

  • Basal body — double-membrane-spanning base made of stacked rings (in IM and OM)
  • Needle/pilus — filament that protrudes from the bacterial surface into the extracellular space
  • Translocon — pore-forming proteins that insert into the host cell membrane (delivered upon contact)
  • C ring + Export apparatus + ATPase — cytoplasmic machinery that powers secretion

How Substrates Are Selected

  • T3SS substrates have a secretion signal in the first ~100 residues (NOT a typical N-terminal signal peptide; it's not cleaved)
  • T3SS chaperones bind the signal region, keep the substrate unfolded and secretion-competent, and guide it to the sorting platform
  • The T3SS ATPase (green in diagram) translocates the unfolded protein through the needle, powered by ATP

Trigger

Following contact with a host cell, secretion is triggered: translocator proteins form a pore in the host membrane, then effectors are injected through the needle into the host.

T3SS Evolved from the Flagellum

  • The basal body of the T3SS and the basal body of the bacterial flagellum share extremely high sequence homology
  • T3SS is thought to have evolved from the flagellum
  • Bacteria can even secrete virulence factors through their flagellar apparatus
Mnemonic: Type III = the "injection" system. It's a molecular syringe (basal body + needle + ATPase) that fires effectors directly into a host cell. Contact-triggered. Evolved from the flagellum.

Type IV Secretion System (T4SS) HIGH YIELD

The T4SS is unique because it can translocate DNA as well as proteins. It evolved from conjugation pili.

Key Features

  • Unique ability among secretion systems: translocates DNA (and proteins) into bacterial or eukaryotic target cells
  • Found in both gram-negative AND gram-positive bacteria, and in some archaea (broadest distribution of any secretion system)
  • Has homology to pili — structurally related to conjugation pili
  • Most common role: conjugation of plasmid DNA between bacteria

Other Roles

  • Bacterial pathogenesis
  • Secretion of transforming DNA
  • Secretion of toxins
  • Secretion of effector proteins required to support an intracellular lifestyle inside a host

Key Components

  • VirB4, VirB11 — cytoplasmic ATPases (provide energy)
  • VirD4 — coupling protein that recruits the substrate
  • Pilus subunit — can extend a pilus to contact target cells

Case Study: Agrobacterium tumefaciens & Crown Gall Disease

  • The T4SS of A. tumefaciens moves bacterial T-DNA across both bacterial membranes
  • Then injects the T-DNA into plant cells
  • The T-DNA integrates into the plant host genome
  • Virulence factors encoded by T-DNA induce tumor-like growths called crown gall disease in host plants
  • This is the basis for plant biotech — we use Agrobacterium to insert genes into plants on purpose!
Mnemonic: Type IV = the "DNA delivery" system. Only secretion system that moves DNA. Conjugation in normal life, plant-genome hijack in Agrobacterium.

Type V Secretion System (T5SS) — Autotransport HIGH YIELD

T5SS is also called autotransport — the secreted protein drives its own secretion. The substrate and its pore are part of the same polypeptide.

Key Features

  • Sec DEPENDENT — relies on Sec to get into the periplasm
  • The substrate and secretion pore are fused into a single polypeptide
  • A single polypeptide drives its own secretion through the outer membrane — "pretty cool!"
  • Does NOT require ATP or a proton gradient — energy comes from the folding of the passenger domain on the outside

The Two Domains

  • Passenger domain — the actual secreted protein. Unfolded in the periplasm, threads through the pore, folds on the outside
  • Translocator domain — transmembrane domain that inserts into the OM as a β-barrel, forming the pore for the passenger to thread through

Mechanism

Type V (Autotransport) — Sec dependent 1. Cytoplasm → [Sec system] → periplasm (whole polypeptide, unfolded) 2. Translocator domain inserts into OM as a β-barrel 3. Passenger domain threads through the pore of the β-barrel 4. Passenger folds on the outside → folding energy pulls the rest through 5. Cleavage releases the mature passenger ("Cleavage occurs here")

Activities of T5SS Autotransporters (8 functions)

  • Adhesion to host cells or extracellular matrix (ECM)
  • Invasion of host cells
  • Immune evasion and serum resistance
  • Contact-dependent growth inhibition
  • Toxicity toward host cells (cytolysins)
  • Intracellular motility
  • Protease activity
  • Auto-aggregation / biofilm formation
Mnemonic: Type V = "auto" transport. The substrate IS the transporter. Sec drops it in the periplasm, then it folds itself out through its own β-barrel pore. No ATP needed — folding is the engine.

Type VI Secretion System (T6SS) — The Phage-Tail Cannon HIGH YIELD

The T6SS is a cell envelope-spanning machine that fires effector proteins into both eukaryotic AND prokaryotic cells. It evolved from phage tails.

Key Features

  • Delivers effectors into eukaryotic AND prokaryotic cells
  • Pivotal role in both pathogenesis (eukaryote target) and bacterial competition (prokaryote target)
  • A single contraction event shoots multiple effectors/toxins into a target cell

Two Main Complexes

  • Membrane complex — made of inner-membrane proteins that are homologous to T4SS components; anchors the system in the cell envelope
  • Tail complex — the firing apparatus, anchored to the cell envelope by the membrane complex; perpendicular to the membrane and extends into the bacterial cytoplasm

Tail Complex = Inverted Phage Tail

The tail complex evolved from a contractile phage tail and has structural elements equivalent to:

  • Tail sheath (TssB–TssC) — the contractile outer sheath
  • Inner tube (Hcp tube) — the tube that gets ejected
  • Baseplate — conformational trigger
  • VgrG spike — tip of the tube; pierces the target membrane
  • ClpV — recycles/disassembles the contracted sheath after firing

Mechanism (resembles phage tail contraction)

1. Loaded state: sheath is extended, effectors bound to spike-tube 2. Trigger: extracellular signal → baseplate conformational change 3. Sheath contracts → inner tube + spike shoots out across target membrane 4. Multiple effectors / toxins delivered into target cell in one shot 5. ClpV disassembles spent sheath for recycling

Comparison to T4 Bacteriophage

The T6SS structure is essentially an upside-down phage tail embedded in the bacterial envelope. Same parts (sheath, tube, baseplate), same contraction mechanism — just pointed outward from the bacterial cell instead of inward toward host DNA injection.

Mnemonic: Type VI = the "harpoon." Spring-loaded contractile sheath shoots a spike-tipped tube into a neighboring cell, delivering multiple toxins at once. Targets bacteria (warfare) AND eukaryotes (virulence).
