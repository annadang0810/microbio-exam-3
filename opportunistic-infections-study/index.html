The Big Picture

This material connects two ideas: why some people are vulnerable to infection (immunodeficiency) and which microbes exploit that vulnerability (opportunistic pathogens). The professor wants you to know:

  • What is immunodeficiency — and which immune components can fail
  • What an opportunistic pathogen is — the three defining traits
  • Three example pathogens with specific virulence factors:
  • MRSA — Protein A, Luk S/Luk F, abscess
  • Pseudomonas aeruginosa — biofilm, quorum sensing

Immunodeficiency HIGH YIELD

DEF Immunodeficiency = a state in which the immune system's ability to fight infectious disease is compromised or entirely absent. A person who has an immunodeficiency is said to be immunocompromised.

Immunodeficiencies can result in autoimmune disorders AND predispose patients to infections — especially opportunistic ones.

The Four Major Components of the Immune System (any can fail)

  • B cells — antibody production
  • T cells
  • Complement
  • Phagocytes

Two Categories of Immunodeficiency

TypeDefinition
CongenitalConditions present from birth
AcquiredConditions acquired during lifetime, NOT present at birth

Type of Deficiency → Type of Infection

Different immune components defend against different microbes — so the type of deficiency predicts the infection risk:

DeficiencyIncreased risk of
B-cell deficiencyPyogenic bacteria (e.g., staphylococci — like S. aureus!)
T-cell deficiencyCertain fungi, viruses, or protozoa

Receptor Deficiency Examples (lecture)

  • TLR-5 deficiency — can't recognize bacterial flagellin → susceptibility to Legionella
  • MBL (mannose-binding ligand) deficiency — failure to activate complement → severe gram-negative infections (e.g., Neisseria)
  • NOD-2 deficiency (cytoplasmic peptidoglycan sensor) → gut immunity defect; involved in pathogenesis of Crohn's disease
  • RIG helicase deficiency — reduced interferon response to dsRNA viruses (e.g., influenza)

Key Concept: SCID Mice

Failures in adaptive immunity (T or B cells) are a primary cause of immunodeficiencies. RAG protein complexes are responsible for antibody and T-cell receptor diversity. Mutations in RAG proteins are how researchers generate SCID (severe combined immunodeficiency) mice for research.

The two-line summary: immunodeficiency = something in the four-component immune system (B, T, complement, phagocytes) is broken. Whether congenital or acquired determines the timeline; which component is broken determines what gets through.

What is an Opportunistic Infection? HIGH YIELD

DEF An opportunistic pathogen = a microorganism that does NOT usually infect healthy hosts but produces infections in:

  • Hospitals (nosocomial infections)
  • Immunosuppressed persons
  • Patients presenting underlying diseases that favor infection

The 3 Defining Characteristics

  1. Usually organisms of LOW pathogenicity — they wouldn't normally make a healthy person sick
  2. They cause serious infections mainly when the host's defense mechanisms are impaired
  3. They can behave as conventional pathogens — but under opportunistic conditions, may cause atypical clinical presentations or disseminated lesions

Why They Need a Dual Lifestyle

Because opportunistic pathogens don't always have a host to infect, they must be able to survive outside of an infection. Two strategies:

  • Live in the environment (soil, surfaces, water) — e.g., P. aeruginosa, C. auris
  • Live as commensals — benefit from colonizing the host but typically don't harm it — e.g., S. aureus in the nose

Hospital Procedures = Opportunity

Hospital procedures (surgery, catheters, breathing tubes, central lines) breach normal host defenses, giving opportunistic pathogens a route in. This is why nosocomial opportunistic infections are so common.

The "opportunity" in opportunistic = a breach (skin wound, surgical site, IV line) or a weakened immune system. Healthy people walk past these microbes every day with no problem.

MRSA: Protein A, Luk S/Luk F, Abscess Formation HIGH YIELD

DEF MRSA = Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus. A lineage of S. aureus that has developed resistance to several β-lactams including methicillin, acquired through horizontal gene transfer.

S. aureus Basics

  • Gram-positive coccoid bacterium that readily clusters — resembles a bunch of grapes ("staphyle" in Greek)
  • Forms colonies with a golden hue on agar — hence "aureus" (Latin for gold)
  • Resides in the nose of 1 in 3 people — harmless there; an opportunistic commensal
  • Causes trouble when moved from its niche (nose/skin) to deeper tissues, e.g., through a skin wound
  • Long co-evolution with humans = impressive immune-evasion toolkit

Virulence Factor 1 — Protein A (immune evasion / anti-phagocytosis)

  • Surface protein on S. aureus
  • Binds the conserved Fc region of immunoglobulins (antibodies)
  • Forces opsonizing antibodies to bind "the wrong way around" — their variable region SHOULD bind the pathogen, but Protein A traps them by their constant (Fc) region instead
  • Result: phagocytes cannot recognize the antibodies properly and don't eat S. aureus
Normal opsonization (works): antibody Fc ←[phagocyte] ... antibody Fab → pathogen With Protein A (broken): antibody Fab ←FREE antibody Fc ←[Protein A on S. aureus] ↑ phagocyte can't bind it correctly

Virulence Factor 2 — Panton-Valentine Leukocidin (PVL): LukS + LukF HIGH YIELD

PVL is a pore-forming toxin that directly kills neutrophils. Two-step mechanism:

  1. S. aureus secretes two proteins, LukS and LukF, that associate with the neutrophil's plasma membrane
  2. Eight subunits of LukS and LukF self-assemble into a ring-like structure that forms pores in the host membrane

When several pores form on the same cell, the neutrophil lyses, triggering inflammation and tissue damage.

Where do MRSA's PVL genes come from?

  • A phage carrying lukS and lukF infected an S. aureus strain — PVL is encoded on a mobile genetic element, NOT in the core genome
  • Combined with another virulence factor (ACME), this produced an epidemic clone
  • S. aureus from the noses of healthy people typically do NOT encode PVL
  • However, ~85% of MRSA strains DO encode PVL

Virulence Factor 3 — Abscess Formation HIGH YIELD

The host releases fibrin (a tough, fiber-like protein) that self-assembles into a protective web as a reaction to the immune-pathogen battle. S. aureus then hijacks this defense:

  • S. aureus secretes coagulase — an enzyme that induces production of MORE fibrin
  • This creates a shell of fibrin around the bacteria — the abscess

Who is really protected?

  • Supposed to be a host defense, but now S. aureus is shielded from:
  • Complement
  • Antibodies
  • Neutrophils
  • Antibiotics
  • S. aureus can survive and replicate inside the abscess; some escape to seed new infections elsewhere
  • Abscesses can form on bone, heart, and other tissues

Why MRSA Is Resistant to Methicillin

  • Methicillin is a β-lactam developed after penicillin resistance arose
  • Within a year, S. aureus evolved a novel penicillin-binding protein, PBP2 (encoded by the mecA gene)
  • PBP2 is an alternative peptidase that cannot be targeted by β-lactams
  • mecA sits on a highly mobile genetic element — transmits rapidly across S. aureus

Bonus: How MRSA spread to skin (ACME)

Normally, S. aureus lives in the warm/moist nose; S. epidermidis lives on the dry/acidic skin. MRSA acquired the arginine catabolic mobile element (ACME) from S. epidermidis:

  • Arc (arginine deaminase system) — counteracts skin acid by generating ammonia
  • SpeG enzyme — inactivates antimicrobial polyamines from skin cells
  • Result: MRSA can colonize the skin — enabling its spread (e.g., the 2003 St. Louis Rams outbreak)
3-part MRSA virulence: Protein A (binds antibody Fc the wrong way around → blocks phagocytosis); LukS+LukF / PVL (8-subunit pore-forming toxin → lyses neutrophils, from a phage, in 85% of MRSA); abscess (coagulase induces fibrin shell → protects from immune cells AND antibiotics).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa: Biofilm & Quorum Sensing HIGH YIELD

P. aeruginosa Basics

  • Gram-negative rod-shaped bacterium
  • Ubiquitous in the environment (soils, surfaces) — the classic environmental opportunist
  • Large genomes (~5–7 Mbp) with one of the highest proportions of regulatory genes in known bacteria — can adapt to diverse environments
  • Leading nosocomial pathogen (hospital-acquired)
  • Intrinsically resistant to a wide range of antibiotics; some MDR strains resistant to nearly all, including carbapenems
  • Causes: pneumonia, bloodstream infections, UTIs, surgical site infections
  • Particularly dangerous for chronic lung disease patients
  • Main cause of morbidity and mortality in cystic fibrosis (CF) patients

Virulence Factor 1 — Biofilm

DEF A biofilm = an assemblage of surface-associated microbial cells enclosed in a matrix.

  • The matrix is made of Extracellular Polymeric Secretions (EPS) that surround and protect cells
  • EPS is composed of: polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and extracellular DNA (eDNA)

The 4 Stages of Biofilm Formation

StageWhat happens
1. AttachmentFree-swimming (planktonic) cells attach to a surface
2. GrowthCells multiply and begin producing EPS matrix
3. Maturation3D structured biofilm forms with channels, layers
4. DetachmentCells slough off / disperse to colonize new sites

What Biofilms Protect Against

Within a biofilm, cells are protected from:

  • Immune factors (e.g., complement, antimicrobial peptides)
  • Immune cells (phagocytes can't reach embedded cells)
  • Predation
  • Antibiotics

Biofilms in the CF Lung

  • CF causes thick, sticky mucus — lungs can't clear infections
  • P. aeruginosa establishes biofilms in the CF lung — persists for the patient's lifetime
  • Host responds with chronic inflammation (recruits leukocytes) but cannot clear the biofilm
  • Chronic inflammation → tissue damage → lung function loss → worsening infection → eventual mortality
  • Predominance of MDR P. aeruginosa in the lung is associated with mortality in CF patients

Virulence Factor 2 — Quorum Sensing

DEF Quorum sensing = the way bacteria communicate with each other. They use a language of chemicals for both inter- and intra-species conversations.

Crucial for regulating group activities:

  • Activating virulence programs
  • Reprogramming metabolic programs
  • Living as a community within a biofilm

The Classic System: V. fischeri / Bonnie Bassler

Quorum sensing was discovered in Vibrio fischeri, the squid light organ symbiont. The mechanism is the textbook example:

At LOW cell density: LuxI enzyme makes autoinducer (AHL, a homoserine lactone) AHL stays diffuse in the extracellular space Not enough AHL to do anything yet At HIGH cell density: AHL concentration rises → diffuses BACK INTO the cell LuxR binds AHL → activates lux operon lux operon → luciferase → light Light helps hide the squid from predators at night

Quorum Sensing in P. aeruginosa

  • P. aeruginosa has FOUR different quorum sensing systems (more complex than V. fischeri's one)
  • Uses these systems to control its actions, including biofilm formation
  • This integration of QS with biofilm regulation is critical for its survival in CF lungs
The link is everything: P. aeruginosa uses quorum sensing to coordinate when to switch on biofilm formation. Single cells can't usefully build a biofilm; only when they sense (via AHL-like autoinducers) that there are enough of them, they cooperatively build the EPS matrix.

Candida auris: Differences, Phenotype Switching, Filamentation HIGH YIELD

C. auris Basics

  • An emerging multidrug-resistant yeast (a fungus)
  • First identified in 2009 in Asia — isolated from the ear canal of a Japanese patient (auris = Latin for ear)
  • Earliest known strain dates to 1996, South Korea (retrospective review)
  • Spreads easily between hospitalized patients and nursing home residents
  • Can be carried on patients' skin without causing infection — allowing silent spread

Why CDC Is Concerned (3 reasons)

  1. Often multidrug-resistant — resistant to multiple antifungal drug classes; some strains resistant to ALL available antifungals
  2. Difficult to identify with standard laboratory methods — can be misidentified (e.g., as C. haemulonii); needs special tests
  3. Causes outbreaks in healthcare settings

Why Thermotolerance Matters — Climate Change Hypothesis

Most fungi can't infect humans because they can't grow at body temp. C. auris's ability to grow at >40°C is hypothesized to be a result of climate change / human-induced global warming. This makes C. auris potentially the first example of a new pathogenic fungus emerging from human-induced global warming.

Phenotypic Plasticity

DEF Phenotypic plasticity = the ability of an organism to change in response to stimuli or inputs from the environment.

  • A common strategy used by microbial pathogens to adapt to diverse host environments
  • Transitions between phenotypic forms allow cells to rapidly respond to environmental fluctuations
  • In C. auris, this includes colony phenotype (white/opaque) switching and filamentation

Colony Phenotype Switching (White and Opaque)

  • A type of phenotypic plasticity where colonies switch between distinct forms
  • Each form has different gene expression and behavior
  • Allows the population to hedge against changing conditions — some cells of one type, some of another

Filamentation in C. auris

Filamentous (hyphal/pseudohyphal) cell growth is normally critical for fungal invasion of host tissues in pathogenic Candida. But C. auris filamentation is unusual:

The 3 Cellular Phenotypes (3-way switching)

  1. Typical yeast cells — locked in the yeast form, UNABLE to filament in vitro even with filament-inducing stimuli
  2. Filamentation-competent yeast cells — appeared in a small proportion after passage through a mouse (systemic infection model). They can be triggered to form filaments later.
  3. Filamentous-form cells — once recovered from the mouse and grown at low temperature (≤25°C), they undergo robust filamentation

Heritable vs Non-heritable Switching

  • Typical yeast ↔ filamentation-competent yeast: RARE event but HERITABLE (a stable epigenetic/genetic switch)
  • Filamentation-competent yeast ↔ filamentous-form cells: NON-heritable, environment-dependent (a reversible response to temperature)

Temperature Switch — Opposite of C. albicans!

Form at 25°C (low)Form at 37°C (body temp)
C. albicansYeast formFilamentous form (used for invasion)
C. aurisFilamentous form (robust)Yeast form — 37°C represses filamentation

This suggests C. auris filamentous forms exist in the environment / on host skin (cooler than inside the host) — NOT used for host tissue invasion like in C. albicans.

Metabolic Difference

  • Genes for sugar transport, glycolysis, and the Krebs cycle are UP-regulated in filamentous cells
  • Suggests filamentous forms have more active general metabolism
  • Supports conclusion: filaments are more important for environmental survival, not infection

Why a 3-way System?

The answer is unknown, but one hypothesis: the 3-way switching system in C. auris (typical yeast, filamentation-competent yeast, filamentous) is more complex and versatile than the 2-way system in C. albicans. The added phenotypic plasticity may help C. auris adapt more efficiently to changing environments.

Don't memorize the 5 differences as a list — build the story: C. auris is unusual because it can survive outside the body (high temp tolerance, salt tolerance, skin/surface persistence) AND switch phenotypes flexibly (3-way). Both adaptations together explain why it spreads so well in healthcare settings.

Common Themes

  • All three survive outside the host (commensal, environment, or surfaces)
  • All three exploit a breach in host defenses (skin wound, mucus clearance failure, indwelling device)
  • Two of three (P. aeruginosa, C. auris) use biofilms to shield themselves
  • All three are major healthcare-associated threats (CDC priority pathogens)

Mnemonic for the Three Virulence Trios

  • MRSA: 3 weapons — Protein A (block antibodies), LukS+LukF (kill neutrophils), abscess (hide from everything)
  • P. aeruginosa: 2 weapons — biofilm (shield), quorum sensing (coordination)
  • C. auris: 2 weapons — phenotype switching (white/opaque), filamentation (3-way switching, environmental form)
